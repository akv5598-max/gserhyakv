## 2.1 INTRODUCTION

The review of literature constitutes the intellectual scaffolding upon which any rigorous scholarly inquiry is constructed. It serves not merely as an inventory of prior research but as a critical dialogue with the existing body of knowledge, enabling the researcher to situate the present study within a broader academic and policy discourse. In the context of this research — which examines the intersection of reservation policy, local self-government, and the lived experiences of Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district of Uttar Pradesh — the review of literature assumes particular significance. The subject matter cuts across multiple disciplines including political science, sociology, gender studies, Dalit studies, development economics, and public administration, making it essential to synthesize diverse theoretical and empirical traditions.

The study of reservation and social change in rural local self-government is located at the confluence of three major intellectual currents in Indian social science: the debate over affirmative action and its outcomes; the scholarship on Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and democratic decentralization; and the growing body of feminist and Dalit feminist scholarship that interrogates the intersectional dimensions of caste, gender, and political power. Each of these currents has generated a rich and contested literature that this chapter seeks to map comprehensively.

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act of 1992, which institutionalized the three-tier Panchayati Raj system and mandated reservations for women (not less than one-third of total seats) and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population, created the institutional conditions for a new generation of political actors in rural India. The entry of Dalit women into the formal structures of local governance represented a historically unprecedented development, challenging centuries-old hierarchies of caste and gender simultaneously. Yet the scholarship on this phenomenon has often remained fragmented — studies of women's political participation frequently underplay the caste dimension, while Dalit studies have not always foregrounded gender with sufficient analytical depth.



This chapter, therefore, attempts a genuinely intersectional review that does justice to the complexity of the research problem. It is organized into eight major thematic sections. The first section reviews the conceptual and theoretical frameworks that inform the study, including theories of representation, affirmative action, and social change. The second section traces the historical trajectory of reservation policy in India from the colonial period to the post-independence constitutional order. The third section examines the literature on Panchayati Raj Institutions, decentralization, and rural governance. The fourth section reviews scholarship on women's reservation and political participation at the grassroots level. The fifth section focuses specifically on Dalit women in local governance, examining both the opportunities and the structural constraints they face. The sixth section reviews the literature on social change and empowerment as outcomes of reservation. The seventh section surveys regional studies with particular attention to Uttar Pradesh. The eighth and final section identifies the research gaps and articulates the theoretical framework that guides the present study.

A note on methodology of the literature review is in order. The present review draws upon a wide range of sources including peer-reviewed journal articles, doctoral dissertations, government reports and committee documents, edited volumes, monographs, and credible working papers. The literature spans the period from the early twentieth century — when the foundations of reservation policy were being debated — to the most recent scholarship published up to 2024. Particular attention has been paid to empirical studies conducted in Uttar Pradesh and the Hindi belt states, given their relevance to the Hardoi district context. The review also engages with theoretical literature from comparative politics and gender studies to provide a conceptually rich foundation for the empirical inquiry that follows.

## 2.2 CONCEPTUAL AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORKS

### 2.2.1 Theories of Political Representation

Any study of reservation in democratic institutions must engage with the foundational theories of political representation. The seminal work of Pitkin (1967) in *The Concept of Representation* remains indispensable to this discussion. Pitkin distinguished between four dimensions of representation: formalistic representation (the authority to act for others, established through elections and authorization), descriptive representation (the extent to which representatives mirror the social characteristics of their constituents), symbolic representation (what representatives stand for or evoke), and substantive representation (acting in the interests of those represented). This analytical distinction has proven enormously productive for subsequent scholarship on minority and women's representation, and it provides a foundational framework for evaluating what Dalit women's presence in Panchayati Raj Institutions actually means and does.

Phillips (1995), in *The Politics of Presence*, mounted a powerful argument for descriptive representation as a matter of democratic justice, arguing that the historical exclusion of women from political life created a democratic deficit that could not be remedied merely by ensuring that existing (predominantly male) representatives pursued women's interests. Phillips contended that there is a distinctive value in having members of marginalized groups present in decision-making bodies — not because group members always speak with one voice, but because presence itself transforms the terms of political debate, raises issues that were previously invisible, and provides role models that reshape the political horizons of excluded communities. This argument is directly relevant to understanding the significance of Dalit women's presence in gram panchayats.

Young (1990), in *Justice and the Politics of Difference*, developed a related but distinct argument, contending that genuine inclusion of marginalized groups in democratic institutions requires not merely formal access but the dismantling of the structural conditions — including cultural imperialism, marginalization, powerlessness, violence, and exploitation — that reproduce their subordination. Young's framework is particularly relevant to the situation of Dalit women, who face what she would call "multiple oppressions" arising from the intersection of caste, gender, and class. Her concept of "oppression" as a structural rather than merely individual phenomenon offers crucial analytical resources for understanding why formal reservation, while necessary, may be insufficient to produce substantive social change.

Mansbridge (1999) further elaborated the theory of descriptive representation, identifying four distinct functions it can serve: providing role models, bringing previously excluded perspectives into deliberation, building trust among constituents, and enabling historically marginalized groups to achieve self-determination over their own lives. Mansbridge's nuanced framework helps explain why empirical studies of women's and Dalit reservation in India have produced mixed findings — the different functions of descriptive representation may be realized to varying degrees depending on institutional, social, and political conditions.



### 2.2.2 Affirmative Action: Theoretical Debates

The theoretical literature on affirmative action is vast and contested, spanning philosophy, law, political theory, and economics. Within political philosophy, two major traditions of justification have emerged. The first, rooted in liberal egalitarianism and associated with Rawls (1971), argues that affirmative action is justified as a means of correcting unjust inequalities that arise from morally arbitrary features such as caste or race. Rawls's difference principle — which holds that social and economic inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society — provides a framework for justifying policies that give priority to historically marginalized groups. The second tradition, associated with recognition theorists such as Fraser (2000) and Taylor (1994), argues that affirmative action is justified not merely on distributive grounds but on the grounds of recognizing the distinct identity and cultural worth of marginalized groups.

In the Indian context, the theoretical debate has been inflected by the specific history of the caste system and the thought of B.R. Ambedkar (1936, 1945), whose analysis of untouchability and caste oppression remains the most intellectually powerful foundation for the case for reservation. Ambedkar argued that the caste system was not merely a system of unequal distribution of resources but a comprehensive system of graded inequality that denied Dalits not just economic opportunities but fundamental human dignity, autonomy, and civic standing. For Ambedkar, reservation was not a temporary corrective measure but a necessary condition for the political empowerment of Dalits — a means by which they could participate in the democratic process on terms of equality rather than being perpetually subject to the benevolence or hostility of upper-caste majorities. Ambedkar's insistence on political representation as a prerequisite for social emancipation provides the deepest theoretical grounding for the present study.

Deshpande (2011), in *The Grammar of Caste*, offers a sophisticated contemporary analysis of caste and inequality in India, arguing that caste continues to operate as a powerful axis of social stratification even in the post-liberalization period, and that reservation policies need to be understood in the context of this ongoing structural inequality. Deshpande's empirical work demonstrates that the persistence of caste-based inequality cannot be attributed to the failure of market forces alone but reflects the continued operation of social norms, networks, and discrimination that systematically disadvantage Dalits across multiple domains of life.

Thorat and Newman (2010), in *Blocked by Caste*, brought together a body of empirical evidence demonstrating the pervasiveness of caste discrimination in contemporary India across labor markets, housing, education, and social interactions. Their collection provides compelling grounds for the continuation and expansion of reservation policies, including those at the level of local governance. The editors argue that discrimination is not merely a relic of tradition but is actively reproduced through everyday social practices and institutional arrangements, reinforcing the case for structural interventions such as reservation.

### 2.2.3 Social Change: Theoretical Perspectives

The concept of social change is central to the present study's concern with the transformative potential of reservation for Dalit women in rural local self-government. Social change refers to alterations in the social structure, cultural values, norms, institutions, and relationships that constitute a given society over time. The literature on social change is theoretically heterogeneous, drawing on structural-functionalist, conflict-theoretical, feminist, and post-structuralist traditions.

From a structural-functionalist perspective, associated with Parsons (1951) and Merton (1968), social change occurs through gradual differentiation and adaptation of social institutions to new functional demands. Applied to the context of reservation, this perspective would suggest that the introduction of Dalit women representatives into PRIs creates new functional roles and demands that gradually modify the institutional culture and social relationships of rural governance. However, this perspective has been criticized for its tendency to underestimate the role of conflict and power in social change.

Conflict theorists, drawing on Marx (1867/1976) and subsequently on Gramsci (1971), argue that social change occurs through the struggle between social groups with opposed interests. From this perspective, the reservation of seats for Dalit women in PRIs can be seen as a site of social struggle in which subordinate groups attempt to use institutional mechanisms to challenge the hegemony of dominant castes and patriarchal power. Gramsci's concept of hegemony — the process by which dominant groups secure the consent of subordinate groups to their own domination through cultural and ideological means — is particularly relevant to understanding why the formal empowerment of Dalit women representatives does not automatically translate into substantive social change; the deeply internalized norms of caste hierarchy and gender subordination continue to operate as forms of what Gramsci called "common sense."

Feminist theories of social change, particularly those associated with intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989, 1991; Collins, 1990), argue that gender cannot be analyzed in isolation from other axes of social inequality including race, caste, class, and sexuality. The concept of intersectionality, originally developed by Crenshaw in the context of Black women's experiences in the United States, has been productively applied to the Indian context by scholars such as Rege (2006), Guru (1995), and Datar (1993). These scholars argue that Dalit women occupy a uniquely disadvantaged position at the intersection of caste oppression and gender subordination, and that their experiences cannot be adequately captured by either a caste-blind feminism or a gender-blind Dalit politics. This intersectional framework is central to the theoretical orientation of the present study.

## 2.3 HISTORICAL BACKGROUND OF RESERVATION POLICY IN INDIA

### 2.3.1 Colonial Origins and the Struggle for Representation

The history of reservation policy in India cannot be understood without situating it within the colonial encounter and the nationalist movement's contested negotiations over the political future of the subcontinent. The roots of reservation as a political instrument lie in the colonial government's decisions regarding separate electorates and reserved seats for various communities, which were formalized through a series of constitutional reforms beginning in the early twentieth century.

The Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909 introduced the principle of separate electorates for Muslims, creating a precedent for community-based political representation that would have lasting consequences for Indian constitutional history. Subsequent reforms extended the principle further. The Montagu-Chelmsford Report of 1918 and the resulting Government of India Act of 1919 expanded the scope of representation and introduced the principle of nomination for depressed classes, a term then used to refer to those who would later be designated as Scheduled Castes. This early recognition of the need for special measures for the most marginalized communities reflected the pressure exerted by emerging Dalit political organizations and leaders, most prominently Ambedkar himself (Galanter, 1984; Zelliot, 1992).

The most dramatic moment in the history of reservation came with the Communal Award of 1932, issued by the British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald, which granted separate electorates to the depressed classes. Ambedkar strongly supported the Communal Award, arguing that only through a separate electorate could Dalits exercise genuine political autonomy, free from the domination of upper-caste Hindu majorities. Gandhi's fast-unto-death in opposition to separate electorates precipitated the Poona Pact of 1932, which substituted reserved seats in joint electorates for the separate electorates granted by the Communal Award. The Poona Pact thus established the framework of reserved seats within a common electorate that has characterized Indian reservation policy ever since (Ambedkar, 1945; Zelliot, 1992; Omvedt, 1994).

The significance of the Poona Pact controversy extends far beyond its immediate political consequences. It crystallized a fundamental tension within the politics of Dalit representation — a tension between Ambedkar's insistence on structural autonomy for Dalit political actors and Gandhi's belief in the paternalistic inclusion of Dalits within a reformed Hindu social order — that continues to resonate in contemporary debates about the efficacy of reservation. As Omvedt (1994) has argued in her magisterial study *Dalits and the Democratic Revolution*, the Poona Pact represented a significant setback for Dalit political autonomy, constraining the choices of Dalit voters and representatives in ways that would persist throughout the post-independence period.

### 2.3.2 The Constitutional Framework of Reservation

The Constitution of India, adopted in 1950 under the chairmanship of Ambedkar as the Chairman of the Drafting Committee, institutionalized a comprehensive framework of reservation as a constitutional commitment to social justice. Articles 15(4) and 16(4) authorized the state to make special provisions for the advancement of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and other backward classes in education and public employment. Article 330 reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the House of the People (Lok Sabha), and Article 332 extended this provision to state legislative assemblies. Article 243D, introduced by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment, extended reservation to PRIs. Article 17 abolished untouchability, and the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955 — later strengthened as the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 — provided legal remedies for the practice of untouchability (Galanter, 1984; Jayal, 1999).

Galanter's (1984) comprehensive study *Competing Equalities: Law and the Backward Classes in India* remains the definitive legal and sociological analysis of India's reservation system. Galanter meticulously traced the evolution of the constitutional and statutory framework of reservation, examining its scope, the principles underlying its operation, and the complex jurisprudence that developed around it. His central argument — that reservation represents a form of "compensatory discrimination" that seeks to rectify the cumulative disadvantages of historical discrimination — has been enormously influential in both legal scholarship and policy debates. Galanter also documented the limitations of the reservation framework, noting that it operated primarily in the domain of formal public institutions and did not address the pervasive discrimination in the private sector and social life more broadly.

Jayal (1999), in *Democracy and the State: Welfare, Secularism and Development in Contemporary India*, situates the reservation framework within the broader project of Indian democracy, arguing that it represents one of the state's key instruments for translating the formal equality of democratic citizenship into substantive social equality. Jayal's analysis draws attention to the tensions between the welfarist logic of reservation — which positions Dalits as beneficiaries of state benevolence — and the democratic logic — which positions them as citizens with rights to political self-determination. This tension is directly relevant to understanding the ambiguous position of Dalit women representatives in PRIs, who occupy simultaneously the roles of beneficiaries of affirmative action and elected representatives exercising democratic authority.



### 2.3.3 Evolution and Expansion of Reservation: Post-Independence Debates

The post-independence decades witnessed both the consolidation and the contestation of reservation policy. The Kaka Kalelkar Commission (1955) and the Mandal Commission (1979–1980) were the two most significant government inquiries into backward class reservation, but their recommendations had quite different fates. The Kaka Kalelkar Commission recommended reservations for Other Backward Classes (OBCs) but its recommendations were not implemented, partly because of disagreement within the Commission itself about whether caste should be used as the criterion for backwardness. The Mandal Commission, by contrast, submitted a comprehensive report recommending 27 per cent reservation for OBCs in central government employment and educational institutions, on the basis of a survey that documented the pervasive socioeconomic disadvantages of OBC communities (Mandal Commission Report, 1980).

The implementation of Mandal Commission recommendations by the V.P. Singh government in 1990 triggered one of the most intense social and political controversies in post-independence India, precipitating violent protests from upper-caste students and generating a vast scholarly literature on the politics of reservation. Béteille (1992), in his influential critique, argued that the use of caste as the criterion for affirmative action tended to reinforce caste identities rather than diminish them, and that a truly egalitarian policy would focus on economic rather than caste-based criteria. This argument has been disputed by scholars such as Deshpande (2011) and Thorat (2009), who argue that caste-based disadvantage cannot be reduced to economic disadvantage and that targeting economic deprivation would systematically underrepresent Dalits, who face forms of discrimination that go beyond poverty.

The feminist critique of reservation policy has highlighted the gendered dimensions of both the design and implementation of reservation. Kishwar (1996) pointed out that the women's reservation within the OBC and SC categories in PRIs created the possibility of an extremely disadvantaged group — Dalit and OBC women — being formally represented without any guarantee that this formal representation would translate into effective political agency. Kishwar's critique, while controversial, drew attention to the importance of examining not just the formal provisions of reservation but the social and political conditions that determine whether reserved representatives can actually exercise meaningful authority.

Kumar (1992), in his study of the social history of the women's movement in India, traces the complex relationship between women's political mobilization and state policy on reservation, arguing that women's organizations have both used and been shaped by the structures of state reservation policy in ways that reflect the broader contradictions of liberal feminism in a deeply unequal society. Kumar's historical account provides important context for understanding the specific provisions of the 73rd Amendment regarding women's reservation in PRIs.

### 2.3.4 The 73rd Constitutional Amendment and its Significance

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (which came into force in April 1993) represented the most significant extension of reservation policy in the post-independence period. By constitutionally mandating the three-tier Panchayati Raj system and requiring states to reserve not less than one-third of the total number of seats in every Panchayat for women — with further provisions requiring that one-third of the seats reserved for SCs and STs should be reserved for SC and ST women — the Amendment created the conditions for a massive influx of women, including Dalit women, into formal political institutions at the grassroots level.

Mathew (1994), one of the earliest analysts of the 73rd Amendment, described it as "a silent revolution" that had the potential to fundamentally transform the character of Indian democracy by bringing millions of previously excluded citizens — women and members of marginalized communities — into the formal structures of governance. Mathew's optimistic assessment was grounded in a belief that formal institutional inclusion, even if imperfect in its initial stages, would generate its own dynamic of empowerment over time as representatives gained experience, confidence, and the capacity to articulate and pursue the interests of their communities.

Rai (2000), approaching the 73rd Amendment from a feminist political science perspective, offered a more qualified assessment. While acknowledging the significance of the formal provisions for women's representation, Rai drew attention to the structural constraints that limited the effective participation of women in PRIs, including the persistence of patriarchal norms within families and communities, the lack of financial autonomy of PRIs vis-à-vis state governments, and the tendency for male relatives of women representatives to exercise actual decision-making power — a phenomenon that came to be described in the literature as the "proxy" or "sarpanch pati" problem. Rai's analysis anticipated a body of empirical scholarship that would document both the possibilities and the limitations of women's reservation in PRIs.

Omvedt (1993) situated the 73rd Amendment within the broader context of the Dalit and women's movements, arguing that the Amendment represented a partial victory for social movements that had long demanded greater participation of marginalized groups in governance. However, Omvedt also cautioned that institutional reform without accompanying changes in the social and economic relations of production and reproduction would have limited transformative potential. Her analysis foreshadowed the empirical findings of subsequent studies showing that the impact of reservation on Dalit women's substantive empowerment varied enormously depending on the social and economic context in which they operated.

## 2.4 PANCHAYATI RAJ INSTITUTIONS AND DEMOCRATIC DECENTRALIZATION

### 2.4.1 The Intellectual History of Panchayati Raj

The idea of village self-government as the foundation of Indian democracy has a long intellectual history, rooted in both Gandhian thought and liberal democratic theory. Gandhi (1962) envisioned the gram swaraj — village self-rule — as the ideal unit of democratic governance, arguing that genuine democracy required the empowerment of the smallest and most basic unit of social life. Gandhi's vision of the village republic as the foundation of a decentralized, non-exploitative social order exercised considerable influence on the Constituent Assembly debates and the early post-independence discussions of local self-government. However, Ambedkar (1948) famously dissented from this vision, arguing that the Indian village was not a community of equals but a site of oppression for the most marginalized groups, particularly Dalits. Ambedkar's critique anticipated the findings of subsequent empirical scholarship showing that the decentralization of power to the village level could, in the absence of protective measures for marginalized groups, simply reinforce existing hierarchies of caste and gender.

The Balwantrai Mehta Committee Report (1957) marked the beginning of the formal institutionalization of Panchayati Raj in independent India, recommending a three-tier system of democratic decentralization. The Mehta Committee's recommendations were partially implemented in various states during the late 1950s and early 1960s, but the early Panchayati Raj institutions suffered from a lack of financial resources, inadequate devolution of functions, and the dominance of politically powerful upper-caste landowning groups (Datta, 1996). Subsequent committee reports — the Ashok Mehta Committee Report (1978), the G.V.K. Rao Committee Report (1985), and the L.M. Singhvi Committee Report (1986) — all identified the fundamental weakness of PRIs and recommended measures to strengthen them, but it was not until the 73rd Constitutional Amendment that Panchayati Raj was given a firm constitutional foundation.

### 2.4.2 Decentralization Theory and Practice

The scholarly literature on decentralization is vast and spans the fields of public administration, political science, development economics, and comparative politics. Theoretically, decentralization is justified on grounds of efficiency — local governments are argued to be better positioned to identify and respond to local needs — as well as democratic participation and accountability. Ostrom (1990), in her landmark work *Governing the Commons*, provided both theoretical and empirical foundations for the argument that local communities can effectively manage common resources through self-governing institutions, a finding with direct relevance to the role of gram panchayats in managing village commons, water resources, and public services.

Manor (1999), in *The Political Economy of Democratic Decentralization*, provided one of the most comprehensive comparative analyses of decentralization in developing countries, arguing that successful decentralization requires not just formal institutional design but the simultaneous devolution of functions, finances, and functionaries to local bodies. Manor's analysis is directly applicable to the Indian case, where the persistent gap between the constitutional promise of Panchayati Raj and its actual implementation has been attributed precisely to the unwillingness of state governments to devolve meaningful financial resources and administrative authority to gram panchayats.

Crook and Manor (1998), in *Democracy and Decentralisation in South Asia and West Africa*, offered a comparative assessment of decentralization in India, Bangladesh, Cote d'Ivoire, and Ghana, finding that decentralization had improved the responsiveness of local government to poor and marginalized groups but had not significantly improved service delivery. Their findings suggest that the political benefits of decentralization — in terms of greater participation and voice for marginalized groups — may be separable from its administrative benefits, a distinction that is important for evaluating the impact of reservation in PRIs.

Bardhan (2002), in a widely cited survey article, reviewed the theoretical arguments and empirical evidence on decentralization in developing countries, concluding that the outcomes of decentralization depend critically on the quality of local institutions, the extent of elite capture, and the degree of genuine accountability of local officials to poor and marginalized constituents. Bardhan's concept of "elite capture" — the tendency for locally powerful groups to seize control of decentralized institutions and redirect resources to their own benefit — is particularly relevant to the study of Dalit women in PRIs, who must contend with the entrenched power of dominant caste elites at the local level.

Bardhan and Mookherjee (2006) refined the analysis of elite capture in decentralized governance, arguing that the risk of elite capture varies depending on the political mobilization of poor and marginalized groups, the competitiveness of local politics, and the degree of transparency and accountability in local decision-making. Their analysis suggests that reservation for Dalit women can serve as a mechanism for reducing elite capture by ensuring that at least some representatives come from the most marginalized segments of the population, but that this effect will be contingent on the broader political and social environment.



### 2.4.3 Functioning of Panchayati Raj in India: Empirical Studies

The empirical literature on the functioning of PRIs in India is extensive and has grown exponentially since the 73rd Amendment. Several major themes have emerged from this scholarship.

The first theme concerns the financial and administrative weakness of PRIs. Mathew and Nayak (1996) documented the persistent gap between the constitutional mandate of PRIs and their actual capacity to function effectively, attributing this gap to the reluctance of state governments to devolve financial resources and administrative authority. Their analysis showed that in most states, gram panchayats depended overwhelmingly on grants from the state government and had little autonomy in setting their own budgets or deciding their own expenditure priorities. This financial dependence significantly constrained the ability of Dalit women representatives to act independently on behalf of their constituents.

Singh and Sharma (2007) examined the devolution of functions and finances to PRIs across different states, finding enormous variation in the extent to which states had actually implemented the provisions of the 73rd Amendment. States like Kerala, Karnataka, and West Bengal were identified as leaders in devolution, while many northern states including Uttar Pradesh lagged far behind. This variation in state-level implementation has major implications for the study of Dalit women in PRIs, as the institutional context within which they operate differs enormously across states.

The second theme concerns the quality of participation and deliberation in gram sabhas. The gram sabha — the assembly of all adult voters in a village — is, in principle, the most basic unit of democratic governance in the PRI system, providing a forum in which all citizens can directly participate in local decision-making. However, empirical studies have consistently found that gram sabhas are poorly attended, dominated by male upper-caste members, and rarely effective as forums for genuine democratic deliberation (Aiyar, Mehta, & Samji, 2007; Jha, Rao, & Woolcock, 2007). The marginalization of Dalit women within gram sabhas reflects both the broader patriarchal norms that restrict women's participation in public life and the caste-based norms that subordinate Dalits in village public spaces.

Jha, Rao, and Woolcock (2007), in their study of gram sabhas in Karnataka, found that participation in these meetings was skewed towards the economically better-off and socially dominant groups, and that the voices of poor and marginalized villagers, including Dalit women, were rarely heard or acted upon. Their study documented the various mechanisms through which dominant groups maintained control over gram sabha deliberations, including the deliberate timing of meetings during peak agricultural labor periods, the physical segregation of Dalit participants, and the social pressure on marginalized members to defer to the views of dominant caste leaders.

The third theme concerns the relationship between PRIs and higher-level government institutions. Besley, Pande, and Rao (2005) examined the functioning of gram panchayats in South India, finding that the effectiveness of PRIs as instruments of redistributive policy depended significantly on the political context — specifically, the degree to which political competition at the local level was genuine and the extent to which political parties were willing to mobilize Dalit and poor voters. Their findings suggest that reservation for Dalit women, while creating formal opportunities for representation, does not automatically generate the political conditions necessary for substantive redistribution.

Sivanna (1990) undertook one of the early empirical studies of Panchayati Raj in Karnataka, examining the social composition of elected bodies and the responsiveness of local governments to the needs of marginalized groups. Sivanna found that despite formal provisions for reservation, dominant caste groups retained significant influence over local governance through informal mechanisms including economic dependence, social pressure, and the strategic deployment of caste and kinship networks. His findings anticipated the consistent pattern documented in subsequent studies of the gap between formal representation and substantive empowerment for SC representatives.

Lieten and Srivastava (1999), in their study of Panchayati Raj in Uttar Pradesh, provided one of the most detailed empirical analyses of the functioning of PRIs in the state directly relevant to the present study. Their work documented the profound influence of caste hierarchies and patron-client relations on the functioning of gram panchayats in UP, showing that the formal provisions of the 73rd Amendment had been largely captured by dominant caste groups who used their social and economic power to control the choices of SC and women representatives. Lieten and Srivastava's analysis of the UP context is directly relevant to understanding the conditions in which Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district must operate.

### 2.4.4 Devolution and Service Delivery in PRIs

A significant strand of the empirical literature on PRIs examines their performance in service delivery — specifically, their effectiveness in delivering public goods such as roads, drinking water, primary health care, and primary education to rural populations. This literature is relevant to the present study because Dalit women representatives' ability to influence service delivery is often taken as a key indicator of their substantive empowerment.

Chattopadhyay and Duflo (2004), in their landmark study of village councils in West Bengal and Rajasthan, used a randomized natural experiment created by the rotation of reserved constituencies to estimate the causal effect of women's reservation on public goods provision. They found that villages with women-reserved seats invested significantly more in drinking water infrastructure — a public good of particular relevance to women, who are the primary water carriers in most rural Indian households — while villages with unreserved seats invested more in roads. These findings provided the first rigorous causal evidence that descriptive representation does matter for policy outcomes, lending support to Phillips's (1995) theoretical argument for the "politics of presence."



Duflo and Topalova (2004) extended this analysis, examining whether women leaders in Indian villages were more or less corrupt than their male counterparts. Using data on road quality and survey-based measures of public goods satisfaction, they found that villages with women-reserved councils had better-maintained public goods — including roads and drinking water infrastructure — and lower levels of corruption, as measured by the gap between official expenditure and actual public goods quality. Their findings challenge the common perception that women representatives, particularly in contexts where they are subject to intense social pressure and have limited political experience, are more susceptible to corruption, and suggest that women's reservation may have positive governance outcomes.

However, these optimistic findings have been qualified by subsequent studies. Dunning and Nilekani (2013) examined the effects of political reservations for Scheduled Castes in India on public goods targeting, finding that SC reservation led to a significant redistribution of public resources towards SC households but did not necessarily improve the overall quality of governance or the provision of universal public goods. Their analysis suggests that reservation leads to a form of targeted redistribution that benefits the represented community but may not transform the overall character of local governance.

## 2.5 WOMEN'S RESERVATION AND POLITICAL PARTICIPATION IN PANCHAYATI RAJ

### 2.5.1 Scale of Women's Political Entry

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment triggered one of the largest experiments in democratic inclusion in world history. Within a decade of its implementation, over one million women had entered the formal structures of local governance across India — a scale of political participation unprecedented in any democracy. By the early 2000s, women accounted for approximately 33 per cent of elected representatives in most states, and in states like Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh that had enhanced the reservation to 50 per cent, women constituted half of all panchayat members (Ministry of Panchayati Raj, Government of India, 2014). The sheer scale of this transformation makes it imperative to understand both its achievements and its limitations.

Buch (2000), in one of the earliest systematic studies of women representatives in PRIs, conducted a survey of women sarpanches across several states and found a complex picture of both achievement and constraint. On the positive side, Buch documented numerous cases in which women sarpanches had successfully implemented development programs, articulated the needs of marginalized groups, and demonstrated effective leadership. On the negative side, she found widespread evidence of the "proxy" phenomenon — the tendency for male relatives, particularly husbands, to exercise actual decision-making authority while nominally reserved women representatives remained figure-heads. Buch's study was influential in establishing the agenda for subsequent research on women in PRIs and in identifying the key variables that determine whether women's reservation translates into genuine empowerment.

Nambiar (2001) examined women representatives in Tamil Nadu PRIs, focusing on the processes of political learning and capacity building that occurred as women accumulated experience in elected office. Nambiar found that while many women representatives initially relied heavily on their husbands or other male relatives for guidance, a significant proportion developed independent political identities and capacities over time, suggesting that reservation, even when it initially produces proxy representation, can generate a longer-term dynamic of genuine empowerment. This finding has important implications for evaluating reservation policies, which are sometimes criticized on the grounds that women representatives are mere proxies without acknowledging the longer-term transformation that political participation can generate.

### 2.5.2 Barriers to Women's Effective Participation

The empirical literature has identified a range of structural, cultural, and institutional barriers that constrain women's effective participation in PRIs. These barriers operate at multiple levels — from the household and community to the institutional and systemic — and they intersect with caste and class in complex ways.

At the household level, the most fundamental constraint is women's limited mobility and autonomy resulting from patriarchal norms of purdah, particularly in northern Indian states including Uttar Pradesh. Kaushik (1993), in her study of women in local self-government, documented how the norm of purdah — which requires women to be veiled and to avoid contact with unrelated men — severely restricted women representatives' ability to attend meetings, interact with constituents, and participate in public deliberations. While purdah norms are more stringent for upper-caste Hindu and Muslim women, Kaushik noted that even Dalit women often face pressure to conform to similar norms as a form of social status aspiration.

Ghosh (2006) examined the specific constraints facing women in Panchayati Raj in West Bengal, arguing that even in a state with relatively strong democratic traditions and left-political mobilization, women representatives faced systematic barriers to effective participation including lack of information about their rights and responsibilities, inadequate administrative support, and social norms that delegitimized women's exercise of authority in public spaces. Ghosh's analysis is particularly relevant to the present study because it documents how gendered constraints on political participation operate even in relatively progressive political environments, suggesting that the situation in more conservative northern states like UP may be more restrictive still.



Mohanty (1995), in her influential essay on women in Indian politics, argued that the entry of women into formal political institutions did not automatically challenge the patriarchal basis of those institutions, and that the transformation of formal representation into substantive empowerment required a simultaneous challenge to the gendered norms and practices embedded in institutional culture. Mohanty's analysis anticipated the subsequent empirical finding that women representatives who were the most effective in exercising independent authority tended to be those who had prior experience with women's organizations or social movements, suggesting that political mobilization outside formal institutions is an important precondition for effective representation within them.

Kishwar (1996) offered a more critical assessment of women's reservation in PRIs, arguing that the provisions of the 73rd Amendment, while well-intentioned, had in practice created conditions for the exploitation of women representatives. Kishwar documented cases in which women sarpanches were required to sign documents without understanding their contents, were excluded from key meetings, and were held responsible for decisions in which they had played no part. While Kishwar's critique was sometimes perceived as an argument against reservation per se, her more nuanced argument was that reservation needed to be accompanied by institutional reforms — including literacy programs, legal aid, and support networks — that would enable women representatives to exercise genuine authority.

Ghatak and Ghatak (2002) used data from West Bengal to examine whether the social characteristics of panchayat leaders — including gender, caste, and educational attainment — influenced local governance outcomes. Their study found significant heterogeneity in the effects of women's reservation on development expenditure, depending on the political context and the educational background of women representatives. These findings suggest that the impact of reservation is not uniform but varies in important ways depending on the characteristics of individual representatives and the broader political environment.

### 2.5.3 The "Proxy" Phenomenon and the Sarpanch Pati Problem

One of the most widely documented and debated phenomena in the literature on women's reservation in PRIs is the so-called "proxy" or "sarpanch pati" problem — the tendency for male relatives of women sarpanches, particularly husbands, to exercise actual decision-making authority while the formally elected woman representative remains largely nominal. This phenomenon has been documented extensively across multiple states and has been used both to criticize women's reservation (on the grounds that it produces "dummy" representatives) and to argue for stronger support measures that would enable women representatives to develop genuine political capacity.

Pai (2001), in her study of Panchayati Raj in Uttar Pradesh, found extensive evidence of the proxy phenomenon, documenting cases in which husbands of women sarpanches attended meetings in their wives' places, signed official documents on behalf of their wives, and made key decisions about budget allocation and program implementation without consulting the elected women representatives. Pai attributed this phenomenon both to the patriarchal norms of the UP countryside and to the structural weakness of women representatives — their lack of education, social networks, and knowledge of administrative procedures — which left them dependent on male relatives for practical guidance.

However, several scholars have cautioned against an overly deterministic reading of the proxy phenomenon. Datta (1998) argued that while proxy representation was undoubtedly a real and significant problem, it was not universal, and that there were many cases in which women sarpanches, despite initially relying on male relatives, gradually developed independent capacities and began to exercise genuine authority. Datta's longitudinal research suggested that the degree to which women representatives functioned as proxies tended to decline over successive terms of office, as they accumulated experience, built political networks, and gained confidence in their own capacities.

Behar and Kumar (2002) examined the proxy phenomenon in Madhya Pradesh, offering a more nuanced analysis that distinguished between different types of proxy relationships. They found that while some women sarpanches were indeed completely dominated by their husbands or other male relatives, others had established more complex and negotiated relationships with their male kin, exercising significant independence in some domains — particularly those directly affecting women's concerns such as health, sanitation, and education — while deferring to male relatives in others. Behar and Kumar's analysis suggests that the proxy phenomenon should be understood not as a binary (proxy vs. independent) but as a spectrum of different forms of gender negotiation within the household and the political domain.

### 2.5.4 Women's Political Participation and Socioeconomic Outcomes

Beyond the question of whether women representatives exercise genuine political agency, a growing body of scholarship has examined whether women's reservation in PRIs has led to improvements in the socioeconomic conditions of women and marginalized groups more broadly. This literature draws on both quantitative methods — including natural experiments exploiting the randomized assignment of reserved constituencies — and qualitative case studies.

Iyer et al. (2012) examined the effects of political reservation for women in India on crime against women, finding that districts with higher proportions of female political representatives had significantly lower rates of reported crimes against women. Their findings suggest that women's political representation has positive effects not just on public goods provision but on the broader social environment affecting women's security and dignity, providing an important additional dimension to the evaluation of reservation policies.



Beaman et al. (2009) used the randomized rotation of reserved gram panchayat constituencies in West Bengal to examine the effect of women's political representation on villagers' attitudes towards women's leadership. They found that exposure to women leaders in reserved constituencies significantly reduced gender-based stereotypes about women's political capacity, particularly among male villagers, and that these attitudinal changes persisted even after the reservation was rotated away. This finding is significant because it suggests that women's reservation has longer-term cultural effects beyond its immediate governance outcomes, gradually transforming the social norms that sustain patriarchal hierarchies.

Drèze and Sen (2013), in *An Uncertain Glory: India and its Contradictions*, situate the discussion of women's political representation within a broader analysis of India's development challenges, arguing that the empowerment of women is both intrinsically valuable — as an aspect of human capability and freedom — and instrumentally important for improving development outcomes in health, education, and poverty reduction. Their capability approach, building on Sen's earlier work (1999), provides a framework for evaluating the impact of women's reservation in PRIs in terms of the expansion of women's capabilities and freedoms rather than narrowly in terms of policy outputs.

## 2.6 DALIT WOMEN IN LOCAL GOVERNANCE: OPPORTUNITIES AND STRUCTURAL CONSTRAINTS

### 2.6.1 The Intersectional Position of Dalit Women

The literature on Dalit women in local governance begins with an understanding of the uniquely complex position that Dalit women occupy at the intersection of caste, gender, and class subordination. As Rege (2006) has argued in her foundational text *Writing Caste/Writing Gender: Reading Dalit Women's Testimonios*, Dalit women's experiences cannot be adequately captured by either a caste-blind feminism or a gender-blind Dalit politics; they require an intersectional framework that acknowledges the specific ways in which caste and gender mutually constitute each other in the lives of Dalit women.

Guru (1995), in a landmark essay "Dalit Women Talk Differently," argued that the dominant strands of the Indian women's movement had systematically marginalized the perspectives and concerns of Dalit women, both by failing to address caste-based discrimination and by positioning Dalit women as passive victims rather than active political agents. Guru's critique challenged upper-caste feminists to recognize that their own privilege was implicated in the structures of oppression that Dalit women faced, and called for a more genuinely inclusive feminism that would center the experiences of the most marginalized. This critique is directly relevant to the study of Dalit women in PRIs, which requires a feminist framework that is equally attentive to gender and caste as axes of subordination.

Datar (1993) examined the specific forms of discrimination and violence that Dalit women face in rural India, including sexual violence perpetrated by upper-caste men as a mechanism of caste control, the denial of basic civic rights, and the exclusion from village public spaces. Her analysis documented how the bodies of Dalit women have historically served as sites for the enactment of caste power — a form of what Sherry Ortner might call "embodied hierarchy" — and argued that this specific vulnerability makes the political representation of Dalit women both urgently necessary and particularly challenging, given the social backlash that Dalit women's assertion of political authority can provoke.

### 2.6.2 Specific Challenges Faced by Dalit Women Representatives

The empirical literature has documented a range of specific challenges that Dalit women representatives face in the exercise of their formal political roles. These challenges are both more severe and qualitatively different from those faced by upper-caste women representatives, reflecting the compound disadvantage of their intersectional position.

Velaskar (1998) examined the educational and social status of Dalit women in Maharashtra, providing important background for understanding the constraints that low educational attainment places on Dalit women's effective participation in governance. Velaskar found that Dalit women had significantly lower rates of literacy and educational attainment than both upper-caste women and Dalit men, reflecting the compound effect of caste and gender discrimination in access to education. This educational disadvantage has direct implications for Dalit women's capacity to navigate the administrative procedures of gram panchayats, read official documents, and engage effectively with bureaucratic officials.

Deshpande (2007), in her study of Dalit women in PRIs in Maharashtra, documented the specific forms of discrimination that Dalit women representatives faced from upper-caste colleagues, officials, and constituents. These included being denied access to the panchayat office, being required to sit separately from upper-caste members at meetings (reflecting the continuing practice of untouchability in public institutions), having their signatures forged on official documents, and being physically threatened or harassed when they attempted to exercise their formal authority. Deshpande's findings illustrate the profound gap between the formal constitutional guarantee of equality and the social reality of caste-based discrimination in rural India.



Nambiar (2001) documented similar patterns in Tamil Nadu, finding that Dalit women panchayat presidents faced systematic resistance from upper-caste men — including the panchayat secretary, officials of the block development office, and male panchayat members — when they attempted to exercise their formal authority. In several cases documented by Nambiar, Dalit women sarpanches who had shown independence in decision-making had faced concerted campaigns to remove them from office, including the filing of no-confidence motions and the lodging of false complaints with higher authorities. These findings suggest that formal reservation, while necessary, does not by itself alter the power dynamics that constrain Dalit women's political agency.

Mosse (1994), in his anthropological study of a Tamil village, provided a detailed ethnographic account of the social dynamics of caste and gender in the context of development interventions, arguing that the formal inclusion of Dalits and women in development programs and local governance structures often reproduced rather than challenged existing hierarchies. Mosse's analysis of how upper-caste groups used the apparatus of formal democratic institutions to maintain their social dominance is directly relevant to understanding the functioning of PRIs in contexts of deep caste inequality.

Béteille (1969), in his classic study of social inequality in a Tamil village, *Castes Old and New*, documented the pervasive influence of caste on every dimension of village social life, from marriage and kinship to economic relations and political power. While Béteille's study predated the era of Panchayati Raj reservation, his analysis of the mechanisms through which dominant castes maintained their social and economic dominance provides essential background for understanding the structural conditions that Dalit women representatives must confront.

### 2.6.3 Agency, Resistance, and Transformation

Despite the formidable structural constraints documented in the literature, a significant body of scholarship has also identified evidence of agency, resistance, and transformation on the part of Dalit women representatives. This scholarship cautions against an overly deterministic reading of Dalit women's political experiences and draws attention to the ways in which even constrained actors can reshape the boundaries of their political possibilities.

Omvedt (1993) documented the tradition of political resistance among Dalit women, tracing the history of Dalit women's participation in the Republican Party of India and in various social reform movements from the Ambedkarite tradition. Omvedt argued that Dalit women have historically been important political actors in their own right, and that the institutionalization of their representation through PRIs built upon, even as it also constrained, this tradition of political activism.

Thorat and Deshpande (1999) examined the impact of reservation in PRIs on the political consciousness of Dalit communities, finding evidence that the presence of Dalit women representatives had contributed to a significant shift in the self-perception and political aspirations of Dalit communities in the areas they studied. Even in cases where individual Dalit women representatives faced severe constraints in exercising formal authority, their mere presence in the panchayat — their occupation of the sarpanch's chair, their attendance at official meetings, their signature on official documents — was experienced by the Dalit community as a powerful symbolic assertion of equal citizenship. This finding is consistent with Pitkin's (1967) concept of symbolic representation and Phillips's (1995) argument about the intrinsic value of the "politics of presence."

Rao (2008), in his field-based study of Gram Sabhas in Karnataka, found that the presence of Dalit women as gram panchayat presidents had in some cases transformed the dynamics of gram sabha meetings, with Dalit community members feeling more confident to attend and participate when the presiding officer was someone from their own community. Rao's findings suggest that descriptive representation at the formal level can have positive feedback effects on the substantive participation of marginalized groups at the grassroots level — a dynamic that Mansbridge (1999) had theorized but that had lacked empirical demonstration.

Buch (2005), in a follow-up study to her earlier work, documented numerous cases of Dalit women sarpanches who had, over time, developed independent political identities and demonstrated effective leadership in advocating for the interests of Dalit communities. These cases included women sarpanches who had successfully challenged the illegal appropriation of Dalit housing land by upper-caste villagers, women who had ensured the implementation of social security schemes for Dalit beneficiaries against the resistance of upper-caste members of the panchayat, and women who had organized Dalit communities to assert their rights under the Protection of Civil Rights Act. Buch's case studies provide compelling evidence that formal reservation, despite its limitations, can generate genuine empowerment for at least some Dalit women representatives.



### 2.6.4 Atrocities and Violence Against Dalit Women in Political Roles

A particularly disturbing dimension of the literature on Dalit women in local governance concerns the violence and atrocities that Dalit women representatives sometimes face when they attempt to exercise their formal authority. The Protection of Civil Rights Act (1955) and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (1989) provide legal protections against caste-based violence, but empirical studies have consistently found that these protections are inadequately enforced, particularly at the village level where local power dynamics favor perpetrators from dominant castes.

Human Rights Watch (1999), in its report *Broken People: Caste Violence Against India's "Untouchables"*, documented extensive evidence of violence against Dalit women, including sexual violence used specifically as a means of punishing Dalit political assertion. The report argued that violence against Dalit women was not merely a reflection of generalized social violence but functioned as a specific instrument of caste control, deployed to punish and deter Dalit political mobilization. This analysis has direct relevance to the situation of Dalit women representatives in PRIs, who occupy positions that challenge the caste hierarchy in particularly visible ways.

Shah et al. (2006), in their comprehensive survey *Untouchability in Rural India*, provided the most systematic quantitative evidence of the persistence of untouchability practices in rural India. Based on a survey of nearly 500 villages across 11 states, Shah et al. found that untouchability practices — including the physical exclusion of Dalits from public spaces, the denial of equal access to water sources, and the use of different vessels for Dalit tea customers — were practiced in an overwhelming majority of rural villages, including in post-independence India. Their findings are directly relevant to understanding the social environment in which Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district must operate.

## 2.7 SOCIAL CHANGE AND EMPOWERMENT THROUGH RESERVATION

### 2.7.1 Conceptualizing Empowerment

The concept of empowerment has become central to both scholarly and policy discourse on the effects of reservation for women and Dalits in PRIs, but it is also one of the most contested and ambiguous concepts in the social science vocabulary. A rigorous review of the literature requires careful attention to the different ways in which empowerment has been conceptualized and measured in different studies.

Kabeer (2001) offered one of the most analytically rigorous conceptualizations of women's empowerment, defining it as "the expansion in people's ability to make strategic life choices in a context where this ability was previously denied to them." Kabeer identified three dimensions of empowerment: resources (the material, human, and social assets that provide the conditions for empowering choices), agency (the ability to make strategic choices and act upon them), and achievements (the outcomes of empowered choices). This three-dimensional framework is directly applicable to evaluating the empowerment of Dalit women in PRIs, and it suggests that the assessment of empowerment must attend to all three dimensions rather than focusing exclusively on formal representation as a resource or on governance outcomes as achievements.

Batliwala (1994) situated the concept of women's empowerment within a feminist framework, arguing that genuine empowerment requires not just the redistribution of resources and opportunities but the transformation of the power relations and social norms that sustain women's subordination. Batliwala's analysis is significant because it challenges the tendency in some policy-oriented literature to treat empowerment as a purely individual or economic phenomenon, and insists on its fundamentally political and social character. Applied to the context of Dalit women in PRIs, Batliwala's framework would suggest that formal reservation is a necessary but insufficient condition for empowerment, which ultimately requires a broader transformation of caste and patriarchal power relations.

Rowlands (1997) further developed the concept of empowerment by distinguishing between three forms: power over (the ability to control others), power to (the ability to take individual action), and power with (the ability to act collectively). Rowlands argued that genuine empowerment involves not just the acquisition of individual power to act but the development of collective power with others, particularly for marginalized groups whose individual action is constrained by structural inequalities. This distinction between individual and collective dimensions of empowerment is relevant to the study of Dalit women in PRIs, where the effectiveness of individual representatives depends significantly on the degree to which they can draw on collective community support.



### 2.7.2 Empirical Studies of Empowerment Outcomes

The empirical literature on the empowerment outcomes of reservation in PRIs has produced a rich but contested body of findings. Different studies, using different methodologies and focusing on different dimensions of empowerment, have reached quite different conclusions about the extent to which reservation has produced genuine empowerment for Dalit women.

Buch (2000) documented numerous cases of women representatives who reported significant changes in their own sense of self-worth, their capacity to speak and be heard in public, and their ability to advocate for their communities following their election to gram panchayats. Buch argued that even in cases where the practical impact of women's representation on policy outcomes was limited, the process of participation itself had significant empowering effects, building women's capacities, networks, and political consciousness in ways that extended beyond their formal role as elected representatives. This argument — that process is as important as outcome in evaluating empowerment — has been influential in subsequent scholarship.

Lieten (1996), in his study of Panchayati Raj in Uttar Pradesh, found more mixed evidence of empowerment, documenting significant variation in the experiences of SC women representatives depending on their individual characteristics, the social composition of their village, and the strength of Dalit community organization. Lieten found that in villages with stronger traditions of Dalit political mobilization, SC women representatives were better able to exercise genuine authority and had achieved more significant improvements in the conditions of Dalit households. His analysis suggests that the empowering potential of formal reservation is mediated by the broader context of political mobilization and community organization.

Agarwal (1994), in her landmark study *A Field of One's Own: Gender and Land Rights in South Asia*, examined the relationship between women's property rights, political participation, and social change. While Agarwal's primary focus was on land rights rather than PRIs, her theoretical framework is highly relevant to the present study. Agarwal argued that genuine women's empowerment requires not just formal political representation but changes in the underlying structure of property rights and economic dependence that sustain women's subordination. Her analysis suggests that the empowering potential of Dalit women's representation in PRIs will be significantly enhanced where it is accompanied by improvements in Dalit women's economic position — including access to land, employment, and credit — and constrained where economic dependence on dominant castes remains severe.

Swaminathan (1990) examined the relationship between women's participation in development programs — including PRIs — and broader social change in Tamil Nadu. Swaminathan found that women's participation in formal institutions had contributed to significant changes in gender norms and relations in some communities, including increases in women's mobility, decision-making power within households, and access to resources. However, Swaminathan also documented the limits of these changes, noting that they tended to be more pronounced in communities with stronger women's organizations and less marked where women's participation in formal institutions was not accompanied by collective mobilization.

### 2.7.3 Role Model Effects and Attitudinal Change

An important dimension of the literature on the empowerment effects of reservation concerns the role model effects of women's political representation — the ways in which the presence of women in visible positions of authority shapes the aspirations, attitudes, and behavior of other women and of communities more broadly. This literature is directly relevant to evaluating the broader social significance of Dalit women's representation in PRIs beyond the immediate policy outputs of their governance.

Beaman et al. (2012) provided rigorous causal evidence of role model effects using data from West Bengal. They found that girls in villages that had been assigned reserved seats for women in gram panchayats — and therefore had had female leaders — had significantly higher educational aspirations and attainment than girls in unreserved villages. Moreover, the attitude gap between parents' aspirations for sons and daughters narrowed significantly in villages with female leaders, suggesting that women's political representation can contribute to broader changes in gender norms within families and communities. While this study focused on West Bengal, its findings are indicative of the potential role model effects of Dalit women's representation in other states including Uttar Pradesh.

Singh (2011) examined the attitudinal effects of Dalit representation in PRIs, finding that the presence of Dalit sarpanches — both male and female — had contributed to significant changes in the attitudes of non-Dalit villagers towards Dalit leadership capacity and civic equality. Singh found that in villages with longer histories of SC representation in PRIs, non-Dalit villagers were significantly less likely to endorse traditional caste-based hierarchies in public life, suggesting that institutional inclusion can gradually transform social norms even in deeply hierarchical societies. Singh's findings provide empirical support for the theoretical argument that formal representation has symbolic and cultural effects that go beyond its immediate governance outcomes.

### 2.7.4 Capacity Building and Training Programs

The literature has also examined the role of capacity building and training programs in enhancing the effectiveness of women and Dalit representatives in PRIs. These programs, provided by state governments, NGOs, and civil society organizations, aim to address the deficit of administrative knowledge, communication skills, and political capacity that often constrains new representatives — particularly those from historically marginalized backgrounds.

Ghosh (2006) evaluated the impact of training programs for women panchayat members in West Bengal, finding that participation in well-designed training programs significantly improved women representatives' knowledge of their formal rights and responsibilities, their confidence in participating in meetings, and their capacity to engage with administrative officials. Ghosh argued that training programs should be seen not as a substitute for structural reforms in the PRI system but as a complement to formal reservation that helps ensure reserved representatives can exercise genuine authority.

Narayan (1999) examined the role of civil society organizations in supporting Dalit women representatives in Andhra Pradesh, documenting how NGO-supported networks of Dalit women representatives had provided crucial mutual support, information sharing, and advocacy capacity for women who would otherwise have been isolated and vulnerable. Narayan's findings suggest that the effectiveness of formal reservation is significantly enhanced where it is accompanied by organized civil society support, and that the development of such support networks should be a priority for policies aimed at translating formal representation into substantive empowerment.

## 2.8 REGIONAL STUDIES: UTTAR PRADESH AND THE HINDI BELT

### 2.8.1 The Political Economy of Rural Uttar Pradesh

Uttar Pradesh occupies a central place in any analysis of reservation and social change in rural India, both because of its enormous size — with a population of over 200 million, it is the most populous state in India — and because of the particular intensity of caste and gender hierarchies that characterize its rural society. The political economy of rural UP has been shaped by the persistence of an agrarian structure dominated by large landowners from upper-caste communities, the historical marginalization of Dalit and OBC communities, and the complex dynamics of caste-based political mobilization since the 1980s.

Pai (2002), in *Dalit Assertion and the Unfinished Democratic Revolution: The Bahujan Samaj Party in Uttar Pradesh*, provided a comprehensive analysis of the political mobilization of Dalits in UP, arguing that the rise of the BSP under the leadership of Kanshi Ram and Mayawati represented a historically unprecedented assertion of Dalit political identity and power. Pai documented how the BSP's electoral politics had transformed the political landscape of UP, bringing Dalit communities into formal democratic politics in large numbers and challenging the dominance of upper-caste parties. However, Pai also noted the limitations of the BSP's electoral success in transforming the social and economic conditions of Dalit communities at the grassroots level, arguing that political representation at higher levels did not automatically translate into empowerment at the village level.

Hasan (1998), in *Quest for Power: Oppositional Movements and Post-Congress Politics in Uttar Pradesh*, examined the broader dynamics of political change in UP, analyzing how the politics of caste mobilization — involving Dalits, OBCs, upper castes, and Muslims — had transformed the party system and created new possibilities for the political assertion of marginalized groups. Hasan's analysis of the structural conditions of UP politics provides essential context for understanding the specific situation of Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district, which is located in a region with a complex and contested history of caste politics.

Lerche (1999), in his study of agrarian change and rural politics in eastern Uttar Pradesh, examined the relationship between caste-based social hierarchy, patterns of landownership, and the functioning of PRIs. Lerche found that in villages where Dalit households were heavily dependent on dominant-caste landowners for agricultural employment, the formal provisions of reservation in gram panchayats did little to alter the underlying power relations, as Dalit representatives were unable or unwilling to challenge the economic interests of their landlord employers. Lerche's analysis highlights the crucial role of economic independence as a precondition for the effective exercise of political autonomy by Dalit representatives.

### 2.8.2 Caste Dynamics and Panchayati Raj in Uttar Pradesh

The literature on PRIs in Uttar Pradesh has consistently documented the profound influence of caste dynamics on the functioning of local governance. Several studies have specifically examined the conditions faced by SC and OBC representatives in UP gram panchayats.

Lieten and Srivastava (1999) provided one of the most detailed analyses of Panchayati Raj in UP, examining the social composition of elected bodies, the financial functioning of gram panchayats, and the political dynamics of local governance across several districts including those in the Hardoi region. Their study found that while reservation had succeeded in bringing SC and OBC faces into formal positions, the actual exercise of authority remained concentrated in the hands of dominant-caste groups who used a variety of mechanisms — economic pressure, social intimidation, bureaucratic manipulation, and selective enforcement of legal norms — to maintain their control over local resource allocation.

Srivastava (2004) examined the specific dynamics of SC representation in PRIs in UP's eastern districts, finding significant variation in the effectiveness of SC representatives depending on the density of SC population in their constituencies, the degree of internal cohesion of Dalit communities, and the strength of connections with higher-level political networks including the BSP. Srivastava found that SC representatives with BSP backing were significantly more able to challenge dominant-caste control of panchayat resources than those without such political backing, suggesting that the effectiveness of reservation is closely tied to the broader political context of Dalit mobilization.

Saxena (1994), in his study of government programs and rural development in UP, documented the pervasive leakages and misappropriations in the delivery of development funds through gram panchayats, attributing them to the capture of panchayat institutions by dominant-caste groups who diverted resources away from Dalit and poor beneficiaries. Saxena's findings are consistent with the broader literature on elite capture of decentralized institutions and highlight the specific challenges that Dalit women representatives face in ensuring that panchayat resources reach their intended beneficiaries.



### 2.8.3 Women in Panchayati Raj in Uttar Pradesh: Specific Studies

The literature specifically addressing women's political participation in UP PRIs has grown significantly since the late 1990s, generating a body of evidence that documents both the specific challenges faced by women representatives in the UP context and the variations in women's political effectiveness across different districts and social contexts.

Kaushik (1993) conducted one of the earliest systematic studies of women in UP PRIs, surveying women gram panchayat members across several districts and finding widespread evidence of the constraints imposed by patriarchal norms — particularly purdah — on women's effective participation. Kaushik found that the social norm of purdah operated as a fundamental barrier to women's political participation in UP, restricting their mobility, limiting their interaction with male constituents and officials, and reinforcing the perception that women's proper sphere was the domestic rather than the public domain. Kaushik's findings have continued to inform subsequent scholarship on women's political participation in UP, and they highlight the particular importance of social norm change as a complement to formal institutional reform.

Pai (2001) examined the specific situation of Dalit women in UP PRIs, arguing that the intersection of caste and gender disadvantage created a particularly severe set of constraints for this group. Pai found that Dalit women sarpanches in UP faced simultaneous challenges from upper-caste members of the panchayat (who resented their presence in a position of formal authority), from dominant Dalit men (who often sought to control the activities of Dalit women representatives through patriarchal household authority), and from the state bureaucracy (whose officials often treated Dalit women representatives with contempt or indifference). Pai's multi-directional analysis of the constraints on Dalit women representatives is one of the most comprehensive available for the UP context.

Mishra (2011), in her study of women panchayat members in Lucknow and Sitapur districts of UP, examined the determinants of women representatives' effectiveness, finding that educational attainment, prior experience with women's organizations, and the support of male family members were the most important predictors of whether women representatives exercised genuine political agency. Mishra's findings are consistent with the broader literature on the individual-level determinants of women's political effectiveness and suggest that policies aimed at increasing the substantive impact of women's reservation need to address these mediating factors.

Singh and Joshi (2008) conducted a comparative study of women's political participation in PRIs across the Hindi belt states — UP, Bihar, MP, and Rajasthan — finding that while all four states had formally implemented the reservation provisions of the 73rd Amendment, the substantive participation of women representatives varied enormously. UP was found to have the worst outcomes in terms of women's substantive participation, which the authors attributed to the combination of extremely patriarchal social norms, the weakness of PRIs as institutions, and the limited organizational capacity of women's groups in UP compared to states like Rajasthan. This comparative finding highlights the importance of state-level factors in determining the outcomes of national-level reservation policies.

### 2.8.4 Hardoi District: Socioeconomic and Political Context

Hardoi district, the site of the present study, is located in the Awadh region of central Uttar Pradesh. It is one of the most populous and economically challenged districts in UP, with significant concentrations of Dalit — particularly Chamar — population alongside dominant OBC groups including Yadavs and Kurmis, and upper-caste landowning families from Brahmin and Thakur communities.

The district's caste demography has historically been characterized by particularly acute forms of caste-based inequality, with Dalit communities concentrated in agricultural labor and traditional occupational castes, and upper-caste and dominant-OBC groups controlling access to land, credit, and government services. The legacy of zamindari landlordism — which was formally abolished by the UP Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950 but whose social effects persisted long after formal abolition — has contributed to a pattern of deeply entrenched economic and social subordination of Dalit communities in the Hardoi region (Lerche, 1999; Lieten & Srivastava, 1999).

Singh (2003) conducted a study of caste relations in Hardoi district, documenting the persistence of caste-based discrimination in access to public services, the practice of untouchability in village public spaces, and the systematic exclusion of Dalit households from local governance. Singh's study found that despite the formal provisions of the 73rd Amendment, Dalit-reserved panchayat seats in Hardoi were frequently controlled by dominant-caste groups through the mechanisms of economic dependence and social intimidation documented in the broader UP literature.

The Human Development Report for Uttar Pradesh (Government of UP, 2006) documented the severe socioeconomic disadvantages of Scheduled Caste communities in the state, showing that SC households had significantly lower rates of land ownership, educational attainment, access to safe drinking water, and income than the state average. For SC women, these disadvantages were compounded by gender-based discrimination, resulting in extremely low rates of female literacy, high maternal mortality, and severe restrictions on women's mobility and autonomy. The socioeconomic context documented in this report provides essential background for understanding the conditions in which Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district operate.



### 2.8.5 Dalit Political Mobilization in the Awadh Region

The Awadh region, of which Hardoi is a part, has a distinctive history of both Dalit social reform and political mobilization. The influence of the Ambedkarite movement in the region dates to the pre-independence period, when Ambedkar's Republican Party of India had a significant presence in Awadh. The subsequent rise of the BSP as the dominant political force among Dalits in UP had a particularly pronounced impact in the Awadh region, where the BSP's blend of Ambedkarite ideology and caste-based electoral politics resonated strongly with Dalit communities (Pai, 2002; Jaffrelot, 2003).

Jaffrelot (2003), in *India's Silent Revolution: The Rise of the Lower Castes in North India*, argued that the rise of OBC and Dalit political parties in UP represented a fundamental transformation of Indian democracy, extending the effective reach of democratic citizenship to groups that had previously been excluded from meaningful political participation. Jaffrelot's analysis of the political dynamics of lower-caste assertion in UP is directly relevant to understanding the context in which Dalit women in Hardoi district pursue their political roles, and provides an important macro-political backdrop to the micro-level analysis of individual experiences.

Verma (2005) examined the social base and political effectiveness of the BSP in Hardoi and neighboring districts of Awadh, finding that the BSP's organizational strength in the region had contributed to a significant expansion of Dalit political consciousness and participation, but that the translation of electoral success into tangible improvements in Dalit welfare had been limited by the persistent social and economic power of dominant-caste groups and the structural weakness of gram panchayats as institutions. Verma's district-level analysis is one of the few studies that specifically addresses the political context of Hardoi, making it an important reference for the present study.

## 2.9 RESEARCH GAPS AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

### 2.9.1 Identifying Research Gaps

The foregoing review of literature reveals a substantial and growing body of scholarship on reservation, Panchayati Raj, women's political participation, and Dalit empowerment in India. However, it also reveals several significant gaps that the present study seeks to address.

**First**, while the literature on women's reservation in PRIs has grown enormously since the 73rd Amendment, the scholarship specifically focused on *Dalit women* as a distinct category — one whose experiences are shaped by the intersection of caste and gender rather than either alone — remains relatively limited. Most studies of women in PRIs treat "women" as an undifferentiated category, obscuring the significant differences between the situations of upper-caste and Dalit women representatives. Conversely, most studies of Dalit reservation in PRIs focus on Dalit men as the normative representative, with Dalit women treated as a secondary or derivative category. The present study seeks to address this gap by centering the experiences of Dalit women as the primary unit of analysis and employing an intersectional analytical framework throughout.

**Second**, the existing literature shows a strong regional concentration in states that have been most active in implementing Panchayati Raj — Kerala, Karnataka, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra. While there are important studies of UP (notably Pai, 2001; Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; and Singh, 2003), the scholarly literature on Dalit women in PRIs in UP is disproportionately thin relative to the state's size and significance. The Hindi belt states, and UP in particular, present a context of particularly severe patriarchal and caste-based constraints on Dalit women's political participation, making them of special importance for understanding the outer limits as well as the possibilities of reservation as a tool of social transformation. The present study's focus on Hardoi district contributes to filling this geographic gap.

**Third**, the existing literature is heavily skewed towards quantitative studies of policy outcomes — changes in public goods provision, crime rates, asset ownership — with relatively little attention to the lived experiences of Dalit women representatives themselves. The subjective dimensions of empowerment — changes in self-perception, identity, aspirations, and sense of agency — have received much less systematic attention than the objective dimensions. The present study seeks to address this gap through a qualitative methodology that foregrounds the voices and experiences of Dalit women representatives, drawing on in-depth interviews and ethnographic observation.

**Fourth**, the existing literature has paid relatively little attention to the *temporal* dimension of reservation's effects — the ways in which its impact evolves over successive terms of office and across generations of representatives. Most studies provide a snapshot of the situation at a particular point in time, rather than tracing the dynamic processes through which formal reservation may (or may not) generate longer-term social change. While a single study cannot fully address this limitation, the present study's attention to the histories and trajectories of individual Dalit women representatives contributes to a more dynamic understanding of the relationship between formal reservation and social change.

**Fifth**, the existing literature has tended to focus on either the individual level (the characteristics and experiences of individual representatives) or the macro level (the aggregate policy outcomes of reservation), with relatively little attention to the meso level — the social dynamics of villages, the role of dominant-caste and Dalit community organizations, and the functioning of gram sabhas as arenas of democratic deliberation. The present study seeks to address this gap by combining individual-level research with community-level analysis.



### 2.9.2 Theoretical Framework of the Present Study

Drawing on the foregoing review of literature, the present study adopts an intersectional theoretical framework that integrates insights from political representation theory, feminist theory, Dalit studies, and the sociology of social movements.

**Intersectionality as the Central Analytical Lens**

The concept of intersectionality, as developed by Crenshaw (1989, 1991) and further elaborated by Collins (1990) and applied to the Indian context by Rege (2006) and Guru (1995), provides the central analytical lens for the present study. The intersectional framework holds that the experiences of Dalit women cannot be understood by adding the analysis of caste oppression to the analysis of gender oppression, but require a qualitatively different analysis that addresses the specific ways in which caste and gender mutually constitute and reinforce each other in the lives of Dalit women. Applied to the study of Dalit women in PRIs, this framework requires attention to the specific forms of discrimination and constraint that arise from Dalit women's dual subordination — forms that are not reducible to either caste oppression alone or gender subordination alone.

**Pitkin's Dimensions of Representation**

Pitkin's (1967) analytical framework of formalistic, descriptive, symbolic, and substantive representation provides a structural framework for analyzing the different dimensions of Dalit women's presence in PRIs. The present study uses this framework to move beyond a simple assessment of whether Dalit women are present in formal institutions (formalistic and descriptive representation) to examine whether and how their presence generates substantive representation — the actual advocacy for and advancement of Dalit women's interests — and symbolic representation — the affirmation of Dalit women's civic equality and political capacity. By attending to all four dimensions of representation, the study aims to provide a more complete and nuanced assessment of the significance of Dalit women's formal representation than is provided by studies that focus exclusively on governance outcomes.

**Kabeer's Empowerment Framework**

Kabeer's (2001) three-dimensional framework of resources, agency, and achievements provides the organizing structure for the study's analysis of empowerment outcomes. The study examines whether and to what extent reservation has expanded Dalit women representatives' access to resources (material, social, and political), their exercise of agency (in decision-making, public participation, and community advocacy), and their achievement of substantive outcomes (for themselves, their households, and their communities). By attending to all three dimensions, the study avoids both the tendency to overestimate empowerment by focusing only on formal representation as a resource and the tendency to underestimate it by focusing only on aggregate policy outcomes as achievements.

**The Social Change Perspective**

The study's concern with social change as an outcome of reservation draws on a synthesis of the conflict-theoretical and social movement perspectives discussed in Section 2.2.3. The study proceeds from the premise that social change is neither automatic nor purely structural — it requires active agents who use available institutional resources and political opportunities to challenge existing power relations. This perspective suggests that the key empirical questions concern not just whether Dalit women representatives have produced policy changes but whether their presence and activity have contributed to broader shifts in the norms, relationships, and power dynamics that sustain caste and gender inequality in rural Hardoi.

**Ambedkar's Political Vision**

Finally, and perhaps most fundamentally, the present study is oriented by Ambedkar's (1936, 1945) political vision of reservation as a mechanism for the genuine political empowerment of Dalits — not merely as beneficiaries of state welfare but as autonomous political agents who exercise democratic power on their own terms. This Ambedkarite orientation shapes the study's commitment to centering the perspectives of Dalit women themselves, its attention to the ways in which formal reservation may be used instrumentally by dominant groups to maintain their power, and its concern with the conditions under which reservation can most effectively serve its intended purpose of generating genuine social transformation.

### 2.9.3 Summary of the Review

The review of literature presented in this chapter has covered a wide range of theoretical and empirical scholarship relevant to the study of reservation and social change in rural local self-government, with particular focus on Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district. The review has examined conceptual and theoretical frameworks of political representation, affirmative action, and social change; the historical development of India's reservation policy from colonial times to the present; the literature on Panchayati Raj institutions and democratic decentralization; the scholarship on women's reservation and political participation; the specific literature on Dalit women in local governance; the empirical studies of social change and empowerment outcomes; regional studies with particular attention to Uttar Pradesh; and the identification of research gaps that motivate the present study.

The review reveals a body of literature that is rich in theoretical insight and empirical detail but marked by significant gaps — particularly in the intersectional analysis of Dalit women's political experiences, in the regional coverage of Hindi belt states, and in the qualitative documentation of the lived experiences of Dalit women representatives. The present study seeks to address these gaps through an empirically grounded, theoretically informed, and methodologically rigorous analysis of Dalit women's experiences of reservation and social change in Hardoi district.

The literature reviewed also reveals the importance of contextual factors — the social composition of villages, the degree of economic independence of Dalit communities, the strength of Dalit political organizations, and the institutional capacity of gram panchayats — in determining whether formal reservation translates into genuine empowerment. These contextual factors will be systematically examined in the empirical chapters that follow, using a combination of qualitative methods including in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and participant observation.
