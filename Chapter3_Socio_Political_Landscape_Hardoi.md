## 3.1 INTRODUCTION

A meaningful empirical inquiry into the experiences of Dalit women representatives in rural local self-government must be anchored in a thorough understanding of the socio-political terrain within which those experiences unfold. The structures of caste, class, gender, and power that shape the everyday realities of Dalit women in Hardoi district are not abstract theoretical constructs; they are material and lived conditions embedded in the specific history, geography, economy, and political culture of this part of Uttar Pradesh. Chapter 3 therefore provides a comprehensive account of the socio-political landscape of Hardoi district, establishing the contextual foundation upon which the subsequent empirical analysis rests.

Hardoi district is located in the Awadh region of central Uttar Pradesh, one of the most politically consequential regions of the Hindi heartland. It is a district of considerable demographic weight, administrative complexity, and social heterogeneity. Its agrarian economy, marked by the legacies of colonial landlordism and post-independence land reform, continues to reproduce structural inequalities along caste and class lines. Its caste demography — characterized by the substantial presence of Scheduled Caste communities alongside dominant upper-caste landowning groups and increasingly assertive Other Backward Class (OBC) communities — makes it a particularly instructive site for studying the dynamics of reservation-based political inclusion. Its political history, spanning the dominance of the Indian National Congress in the early post-independence decades through the revolutionary transformation wrought by the Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP) from the 1990s onwards, illuminates both the possibilities and the limits of Dalit political assertion in a deeply stratified social order.

This chapter is organized into ten substantive sections. Section 3.2 provides the geographical and administrative profile of Hardoi district. Section 3.3 examines the demographic composition of the district, drawing on census data to establish the population characteristics that form the backdrop to the study. Section 3.4 analyzes the caste structure and social hierarchy of Hardoi, with particular attention to the position of Scheduled Caste communities within the local caste order. Section 3.5 examines the economic structure, patterns of land ownership, and agrarian relations that constitute the material basis of social inequality in the district. Section 3.6 reviews the educational status and literacy patterns of the district's population, with special attention to the educational disadvantage of Dalit women. Section 3.7 traces the political history and electoral dynamics of Hardoi, examining how caste-based political mobilization has shaped the district's political landscape. Section 3.8 describes the structure and functioning of Panchayati Raj Institutions in Hardoi, providing the institutional context within which Dalit women representatives operate. Section 3.9 provides a detailed profile of the Scheduled Caste communities of Hardoi, examining their social, economic, and political conditions. Section 3.10 focuses specifically on Dalit women in Hardoi, profiling the intersecting disadvantages they face and documenting evidence of their emerging political agency. Section 3.11 synthesizes the foregoing analysis and draws out its significance for the empirical inquiry that follows.

## 3.2 GEOGRAPHICAL AND ADMINISTRATIVE PROFILE OF HARDOI DISTRICT

### 3.2.1 Location and Physical Setting

Hardoi district is situated in the central part of Uttar Pradesh, lying between 26°44' and 27°44' North latitude and 79°41' and 80°44' East longitude. It forms part of the Gangetic plains, one of the most densely populated and agriculturally productive regions of South Asia. The district is bounded to the north by Shahjahanpur district, to the northeast by Lakhimpur Kheri district, to the east by Sitapur district, to the south by Unnao and Kanpur Dehat districts, and to the west by Farrukhabad and Hardoi-Kannauj districts. Its geographical position places it at the heart of the Awadh region, a culturally and historically significant zone that has shaped the political and social character of central Uttar Pradesh for centuries (District Gazetteer, Hardoi, 2002; Census of India, 2011).

The district covers a total geographical area of approximately 5,986 square kilometres, making it one of the medium-sized districts in Uttar Pradesh. The terrain is predominantly flat alluvial plain, characteristic of the upper Gangetic doab, with the Ganga and its tributaries — the Ramganga, the Gomti, the Sai, and several smaller streams — draining the district. This riverine geography has historically supported intensive agriculture, particularly the cultivation of rice, wheat, sugarcane, and pulses. However, periodic flooding of the low-lying areas near the rivers has also created zones of ecological vulnerability, disproportionately affecting the landless and near-landless households, many of which belong to Scheduled Caste communities (Directorate of Economics and Statistics, UP, 2015; Hardoi District Gazetteer, 2002).

The district experiences a sub-tropical monsoon climate, with hot summers (April to June), a monsoon season (July to September), and cool winters (November to February). Average annual rainfall is approximately 850–950 mm, concentrated in the monsoon months. The agricultural calendar is shaped by this climatic pattern, with the kharif (autumn) and rabi (spring) crop cycles determining the rhythm of rural life and the availability of agricultural wage labor — a critical determinant of the economic position of landless Dalit households.

### 3.2.2 Administrative Divisions

For administrative purposes, Hardoi district is organized into a hierarchical structure of tehsils, development blocks, and revenue villages. At the time of the 2011 Census, the district comprised six tehsils: Hardoi, Sandila, Shahabad, Sawan Lal, Bilgram, and Madhoganj. The district is further divided into nineteen development blocks (vikaskhand), which serve as the primary units for the delivery of government development programs and the administration of Panchayati Raj Institutions. The nineteen blocks are: Hardoi, Sandila, Shahabad, Bilgram, Sawan Lal, Madhoganj, Kachhauna, Pali, Tandiyawan, Bharkhani, Sandi, Tikait Nagar, Ahirori, Sursa, Gopamau, Mallanwan, Pihani, Todarpur, and Balamau (District Administration Hardoi, 2020; Census of India, 2011).

The district contains a total of 1,981 revenue villages, of which 1,929 are inhabited. This large number of villages, distributed across a relatively flat terrain, presents significant administrative challenges for the delivery of government services and the effective functioning of Panchayati Raj Institutions. The district headquarters is the town of Hardoi, which is connected to Lucknow (the state capital, approximately 100 km to the east) and to Kanpur (approximately 85 km to the south) by road and rail. This connectivity to major urban centres has important implications for the flow of political information, the availability of legal aid and civil society support, and the access of Dalit women representatives to higher administrative authorities (District Administration Hardoi, 2020).

### 3.2.3 Urban-Rural Distribution

Hardoi district is overwhelmingly rural in character. According to the 2011 Census, approximately 93.7 per cent of the district's population lived in rural areas, with only 6.3 per cent in urban areas. This high degree of rurality is significant for the present study, as it means that the gram panchayat is the primary — and in many cases the only — formal governmental institution with which the majority of the district's inhabitants interact. The gram panchayat's role in delivering public services, maintaining records, implementing government schemes, and adjudicating local disputes is therefore of enormous practical importance to rural households, particularly those from marginalized communities whose access to urban government offices and legal institutions is severely constrained by poverty, illiteracy, and social exclusion (Census of India, 2011; Government of Uttar Pradesh, 2006).

The district has three urban areas of significance: the district headquarters town of Hardoi, and the smaller towns of Sandila and Shahabad. These urban centres have some manufacturing and trading activity but are primarily administrative and commercial hubs serving the surrounding rural hinterland. The limited urbanization of Hardoi district, combined with its dependence on agriculture and the concentration of Scheduled Caste households in landless agricultural labor, reinforces the structural conditions of rural dependence and caste-based hierarchy that the present study examines.

### 3.2.4 Transport and Connectivity

The district's transport infrastructure, while improved in recent decades, remains uneven in its distribution. The principal highway connecting Lucknow to Delhi (National Highway 27) passes through the district, and there are several state highways connecting the district headquarters to the tehsil towns and major development blocks. However, many of the more remote revenue villages — particularly those in the northern and eastern parts of the district, which have higher concentrations of Scheduled Caste households — are accessible only by unpaved roads, which become impassable during the monsoon season. This physical inaccessibility has important consequences for the exercise of political representation by Dalit women panchayat members, who may find it practically impossible to reach block-level offices or district headquarters during critical periods (District Administration Hardoi, 2020; Pandey, 2009).

The limited connectivity of many Dalit-dominated hamlets (tolas) to the main village roads and to the panchayat bhavan (office) is itself a spatial manifestation of caste segregation, as the residential areas of Dalit communities are typically located on the periphery of villages, often at some distance from the village centre where upper-caste households and public institutions are concentrated. This spatial marginalization compounds the social and political marginalization of Dalit representatives and their constituents, creating additional practical barriers to the effective exercise of formal political authority (Shah et al., 2006; Srinivas, 1994).

## 3.3 DEMOGRAPHIC PROFILE AND POPULATION CHARACTERISTICS

### 3.3.1 Total Population and Growth

According to the Census of India 2011, Hardoi district had a total population of 4,091,380 persons, making it one of the most populous districts in Uttar Pradesh and, by extension, one of the most populous administrative units in India. The district's population had grown from 3,507,693 in 2001, representing a decadal growth rate of approximately 16.6 per cent — slightly below the state average growth rate of 20.2 per cent for the same period, but still reflecting substantial population pressure on the district's limited economic and natural resources (Census of India, 2011; Office of the Registrar General of India, 2013).

The total population in 2011 comprised 2,157,153 males and 1,934,227 females, yielding a sex ratio of 897 females per 1,000 males. This sex ratio was lower than the national average of 943 and somewhat below the state average of 912, indicating a relatively more pronounced gender imbalance in Hardoi compared to the broader national and state contexts. The unfavourable sex ratio reflects a combination of factors including the practice of sex-selective abortion, higher female mortality rates attributable to differential access to nutrition and healthcare, and the social devaluation of girl children that characterizes patriarchal rural communities (Census of India, 2011; Kishor, 1993). For Dalit women, these demographic disadvantages interact with caste-based discrimination to create compounded vulnerabilities.

The child sex ratio (0–6 years) in Hardoi in 2011 was 880 girls per 1,000 boys, compared to the national average of 919 and the state average of 902. This particularly adverse child sex ratio suggests that son preference and related practices of female discrimination are especially pronounced in Hardoi, reflecting deeply entrenched patriarchal norms that have significant implications for the valuation of women's lives, their access to education and healthcare, and ultimately for the prospects of women's political empowerment (Census of India, 2011; Drèze & Khera, 2000).

### 3.3.2 Population Density and Distribution

With a geographical area of approximately 5,986 square kilometres and a population of over 4 million, Hardoi district has a population density of approximately 684 persons per square kilometre — considerably higher than the national average of 382 and slightly above the state average of 828. This high population density, combined with the predominantly agrarian economy and limited non-agricultural employment opportunities, creates intense competition for land, water, and livelihood resources that exacerbates existing social inequalities and fuels caste-based conflicts over access to common property resources and government welfare schemes (Census of India, 2011; Beteille, 1965).

The distribution of population across the district is uneven, with the blocks adjacent to the district headquarters and the principal highways having higher population densities than the more remote northern and northeastern blocks. The northern blocks of the district, particularly those bordering Lakhimpur Kheri and Shahjahanpur, have historically been areas of lower agricultural productivity due to waterlogging and flooding, and have correspondingly higher concentrations of poverty, illiteracy, and social disadvantage. Notably, these areas also tend to have higher proportions of Scheduled Caste households, reflecting the historical pattern by which Dalit communities were settled on the least productive and most ecologically vulnerable land (Lerche, 1999; Singh, 2003).

### 3.3.3 Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe Population

The Scheduled Caste population of Hardoi district, as recorded in the 2011 Census, was 1,213,424 persons, constituting approximately 29.7 per cent of the total population. This is significantly higher than the state average of 20.7 per cent and the national average of 16.6 per cent for Scheduled Castes, making Hardoi one of the districts with the highest SC concentration in Uttar Pradesh (Census of India, 2011; Office of the Registrar General of India, 2013). The high SC proportion has important political implications: it means that in most assembly and panchayat constituencies, SC voters constitute a substantial bloc whose political preferences can decisively influence electoral outcomes. This demographic weight has been a key factor in the electoral strategy of the Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP) and has shaped the dynamics of political competition in Hardoi (Pai, 2002; Jaffrelot, 2003).

The Scheduled Tribe population of Hardoi is negligible, comprising less than 0.1 per cent of the total population, as is characteristic of most plains districts of Uttar Pradesh. The social landscape of Hardoi is thus shaped primarily by the dynamics of caste rather than the tribal-non-tribal cleavages that characterize other regions of India.

### 3.3.4 Age Structure and Dependency

The age structure of Hardoi's population reflects the demographic profile typical of economically underdeveloped rural districts, with a young age pyramid and a high dependency ratio. Approximately 37.8 per cent of the population was below 15 years of age in 2011, reflecting high fertility rates and limited demographic transition. The working-age population (15–59 years) constituted approximately 55.7 per cent of the total, while those aged 60 and above accounted for approximately 6.5 per cent (Census of India, 2011).

This young age structure has significant implications for the district's future political trajectory: the large cohort of young Dalits who have grown up in the post-Mandal era, with greater exposure to Ambedkarite ideology through the BSP's political mobilization, are likely to have different political aspirations and orientations than older generations shaped by the patronage politics of the Congress era. The emergence of younger Dalit women with higher levels of education and political consciousness as candidates for panchayat seats reflects this generational shift, which the present study takes note of in its analysis of the trajectories of Dalit women representatives (Pai, 2002; Verma, 2005).

### 3.3.5 Religious Composition

The population of Hardoi is predominantly Hindu, with a significant Muslim minority. According to the 2011 Census, Hindus constituted approximately 83.2 per cent of the population and Muslims approximately 16.5 per cent, with other religious communities accounting for less than 0.3 per cent. The Muslim population is concentrated primarily in the towns and in several rural areas with historical traditions of artisanal manufacture and trade. The religious composition of the district is significant for understanding political dynamics, as both the Congress and the BSP have at different times sought to build Hindu-Muslim-Dalit alliances, while the BJP has mobilized along Hindu communal lines (Hasan, 1998; Jaffrelot, 2003).

For the purposes of the present study, it is important to note that the overwhelming majority of the Scheduled Caste population of Hardoi is Hindu — specifically, they belong to Hindu Dalit castes such as Chamar, Pasi, Dhobi, Kori, Balmiki, and Bind. A small proportion of former untouchable communities have converted to Buddhism, following the path laid by Ambedkar's 1956 conversion, and there are also communities of Muslim Dalits (sometimes called "Ajlaf") who face discrimination within the Muslim community, though these are not constitutionally designated as Scheduled Castes (Omvedt, 1994; Ambedkar, 1948).

## 3.4 CASTE STRUCTURE AND SOCIAL HIERARCHY IN HARDOI

### 3.4.1 The Caste Order in the Awadh Region

The caste structure of Hardoi district must be understood within the broader context of the caste system as it has historically operated in the Awadh region. The Awadh region was characterized under colonial rule by a distinctive agrarian structure, the taluqdari system, in which large estates were controlled by a relatively small number of high-caste landlords (taluqdars), most of whom were Thakurs (Rajputs) or Brahmins, while the vast majority of the rural population — including both lower-caste Hindus and Muslims — worked as dependent cultivators or agricultural laborers (Mukherjee, 1958; Pandey, 1978). This structure of agrarian hierarchy, which combined caste dominance with economic exploitation, produced a particular pattern of social relations in Hardoi in which the authority of the dominant castes was simultaneously economic, social, religious, and political.

The post-independence period brought formal legal changes — the abolition of zamindari, universal adult suffrage, and ultimately the reservation provisions of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment — but the social relations and cultural norms embedded in the centuries-old caste order have proven remarkably resilient. As Srinivas (1966) noted in his foundational work on caste and social change, the dominant caste in a given village context exercises authority not merely through economic ownership but through the control of social norms, ritual hierarchy, and the mechanisms of social sanctions against those who challenge the established order. In Hardoi, as in much of the Awadh region, this pattern of dominant-caste authority has adapted to the democratic context by employing formal democratic institutions as new arenas for the reproduction of old hierarchies (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Singh, 2003).

### 3.4.2 Upper-Caste Communities: Brahmin and Thakur Dominance

The upper-caste groups in Hardoi consist primarily of Brahmins and Thakurs (Rajputs), with smaller numbers of Baniyas (trading castes) and Kayasthas (scribal castes). Brahmins and Thakurs have historically been the dominant landowners in the district, controlling the most productive agricultural land and exercising social authority through their positions as village priests, moneylenders, large farmers, and local political intermediaries. Despite the formal abolition of zamindari through the UP Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950, land redistribution in Hardoi was incomplete and uneven, and many Brahmin and Thakur families retained substantial landholdings through various mechanisms of evasion and adaptation (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Mukherjee, 1958).

The political influence of Brahmin and Thakur communities in Hardoi has historically been channelled through the Congress party and, in the post-Mandal period, through the BJP, which has successfully presented itself as a defender of upper-caste interests against the perceived encroachments of reservation policies. In the context of panchayat politics, upper-caste communities in Hardoi typically retain significant informal influence over local governance even when formal elected positions are occupied by SC or OBC representatives, through a combination of economic leverage over dependent households, control over the panchayat secretary and block-level administrative officials, and social pressure on Dalit representatives to defer to upper-caste preferences (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Saxena, 1994).

### 3.4.3 Other Backward Classes: Yadav, Kurmi, and Other Communities

The Other Backward Class (OBC) population of Hardoi is substantial and internally diverse. The principal OBC communities include Yadavs, Kurmis (Patels), Lodhs, Koeris, Mauryas, and numerous smaller artisanal and cultivating castes. Among these, Yadavs are the most politically organized, having become the social base of the Samajwadi Party (SP) from the 1990s onwards. The political mobilization of Yadavs under the SP has significantly altered the balance of social power in UP, challenging upper-caste dominance and creating new forms of competition between Yadav and Dalit communities for political office and government resources (Hasan, 1998; Jaffrelot, 2003).

In Hardoi, the relationship between Yadav/OBC communities and Scheduled Caste communities is complex and often contentious. While the Mandal Commission's recommendations formally created a framework of common interest between OBCs and Dalits as beneficiaries of reservation, the actual social relations between these groups in Hardoi villages are frequently marked by tension and hierarchy. In many villages of the district, Yadav families have emerged as the new dominant caste at the local level, having acquired land through post-zamindari reforms and political connections, and some Yadav landlords exercise a relationship of economic and social dominance over Dalit agricultural laborers that is structurally similar to the dominance previously exercised by Brahmin and Thakur zamindars (Lerche, 1999; Singh, 2003).

Kurmi (Patel/Maurya) communities in Hardoi are generally more prosperous than Yadavs in terms of agricultural landholding and are known for their organizational capacity through caste associations. They have been associated with both the SP and, more recently, the BJP, and their relationship with Dalit communities is typically characterized by paternalistic patronage rather than overt hostility. However, Kurmis also compete with Dalits for OBC-reserved positions in panchayats and other institutions, creating potential conflicts of interest that Dalit women representatives must navigate carefully.

### 3.4.4 Scheduled Caste Communities: Diversity and Internal Hierarchy

The Scheduled Caste population of Hardoi is not monolithic but comprises numerous distinct jati (caste) groups, each with its own occupational identity, internal hierarchy, and political orientation. The principal SC communities in the district include:

**Chamars (Jatavs)**: The largest SC community in Hardoi and in Uttar Pradesh more broadly, Chamars are traditionally associated with leatherwork and now predominantly work as agricultural laborers, construction workers, and in urban informal sector employment. Chamars have been the primary social base of the BSP in Hardoi, and their political consciousness has been most strongly shaped by Ambedkarite ideology. Many of the Dalit women who have entered panchayat politics in Hardoi as BSP candidates or BSP-supported independent candidates are from Chamar households (Pai, 2002; Mendelsohn & Vicziany, 1998).

**Pasis**: The second-largest SC community in Hardoi, Pasis are traditionally associated with pig-rearing and the manufacture of country liquor (toddy). They occupy a somewhat intermediate position within the SC hierarchy, being regarded by Chamars as socially lower and by themselves as having a distinct and proud martial heritage associated with the Pasi king Maharaja Bijli Pasi. Pasis have historically been the social base of the Republican Party of India and, later, the BSP, but their political loyalty has been more volatile than that of Chamars (Pai, 2002; Singh, 2003).

**Dhobis**: Traditionally washermen, Dhobis constitute a smaller but distinct SC community in Hardoi. Their occupational identity as washers of clothes for upper-caste households placed them in a relationship of jajmani (patron-client) dependence that is gradually eroding as market-based laundry services replace traditional arrangements.

**Koris and Julahis**: Communities associated with weaving, Koris are Hindu and Julahis are Muslim, though only the former are designated as Scheduled Castes. Kori weavers in Hardoi have been partially integrated into the handloom textile economy but have faced severe economic distress as power-loom production has undercut traditional handloom weaving.

**Balmikis (Valmikis)**: Associated with sanitation work and scavenging, Balmikis occupy the lowest position in the Scheduled Caste hierarchy and face the most severe forms of social stigma and discrimination. Despite constitutional provisions and the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act (2013), manual scavenging has not been entirely eliminated in Hardoi, and Balmiki communities continue to face extreme social exclusion (Human Rights Watch, 2014; Wilson, 2016).

The internal differentiation of the SC community has important implications for panchayat politics in Hardoi. The division of SC reserved seats among different SC communities, the competition between Chamars and Pasis for BSP nominations, and the tensions between SC communities over access to government housing schemes and welfare benefits create internal conflicts that can undermine the collective political agency of the SC population and make it easier for dominant-caste groups to divide and rule (Singh, 2003; Pai, 2002).

### 3.4.5 Caste and Spatial Segregation

A crucial dimension of the caste structure in Hardoi is its spatial expression in village settlement patterns. In the overwhelming majority of villages in Hardoi, Scheduled Caste households are settled in spatially distinct hamlets (tolas or purwas) physically separated from the main village and from the residential areas of upper-caste and OBC households. This pattern of spatial segregation — a direct expression of the purity-pollution norms of the caste system — means that Dalit communities typically lack access to the common resources located in the village centre, including the main road, the well or handpump, the temple, the panchayat building, and the school (Shah et al., 2006; Mendelsohn & Vicziany, 1998).

The spatial separation of Dalit tolas from the main village has immediate practical consequences for the exercise of political authority by Dalit women panchayat members. When a Dalit woman sarpanch attempts to hold a gram sabha meeting, convene the panchayat, or inspect a public works project, she must negotiate the social norms of an integrated village space in which her physical presence in the upper-caste area may itself be perceived as a transgression of the established social order. The literature on caste and village life in Uttar Pradesh consistently documents how this spatial dimension of caste segregation reinforces the social distance between Dalit representatives and the dominant-caste members of their gram panchayats (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Shah et al., 2006).

### 3.4.6 Caste Violence and Social Control in Hardoi

The caste hierarchy in Hardoi is maintained not only through economic dependence and social norms but also through the threat and use of violence. Data from the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) consistently show that Uttar Pradesh records among the highest numbers of cases registered under the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, in the country. Hardoi district itself has been the site of documented cases of violence against Dalits, including physical assault, destruction of property, sexual violence against Dalit women, and social boycotts of Dalit households that challenge dominant-caste authority (NCRB, 2019; Human Rights Watch, 1999).

For Dalit women in political roles, the risk of caste-motivated violence is particularly acute. When a Dalit woman sarpanch attempts to exercise her formal authority — by refusing to sign fraudulent works orders, by demanding that development funds be directed to Dalit households, or by asserting her right to preside over gram sabha meetings — she faces the possibility of violent retaliation from upper-caste or dominant-OBC men who resent her assertion of formal authority. Several field studies of Dalit women panchayat members in UP have documented cases of physical threats, public humiliation, and sexual harassment directed at Dalit women representatives who challenged the entrenched caste and gender order (Pai, 2001; Deshpande, 2007). This context of potential violence constitutes a pervasive backdrop to the political experiences of Dalit women in Hardoi.

## 3.5 ECONOMIC STRUCTURE, LAND RELATIONS AND AGRARIAN CONTEXT

### 3.5.1 The Agrarian Economy of Hardoi

Hardoi district is fundamentally an agrarian economy. Agriculture and allied activities provide the primary livelihood for the majority of the district's working population, and the structure of agricultural production — the pattern of land ownership, the organization of cultivation, and the terms of agricultural labor — is the single most important determinant of the social and economic position of different communities in the district. Understanding the agrarian structure of Hardoi is therefore essential for understanding the material conditions within which Dalit women pursue their political roles as panchayat members.

According to data from the Agricultural Census of India (2015–16) and the Directorate of Economics and Statistics, Uttar Pradesh, the total cultivable area of Hardoi district is approximately 4,63,000 hectares, of which approximately 85 per cent is under cultivation. The principal crops are wheat, paddy, sugarcane, pulses, and vegetables. The district has reasonable irrigation infrastructure, with a significant proportion of cultivated area served by government canals (primarily the Sarda and Upper Ganga canal systems) and by privately owned tube-wells. However, access to irrigation is highly unequal: large and medium farmers with secure land tenure and capital are able to invest in and maintain tube-wells, while small and marginal farmers and tenant cultivators — a category that disproportionately includes SC households — are dependent on public canals whose maintenance has deteriorated significantly under conditions of government fiscal pressure (Directorate of Economics and Statistics, UP, 2015; Agricultural Census of India, 2015–16).

### 3.5.2 Land Ownership and the Legacy of Zamindari

The pattern of land ownership in Hardoi continues to be marked by the legacy of the zamindari system that was formally abolished by the Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of 1950. This landmark legislation sought to transfer ownership of agricultural land to the actual tillers (peasant cultivators) by abolishing the intermediary rights of zamindars (large landlords). However, as numerous studies have documented, the implementation of zamindari abolition in UP was severely compromised by the political influence of zamindari families, many of which succeeded in retaining large landholdings through a combination of benami (nominal) transfers, manipulation of land records, and political connections with the Congress party that governed UP in the early post-independence decades (Mukherjee, 1958; Brass, 1974; Lieten & Srivastava, 1999).

For Scheduled Caste households in Hardoi, the land reform legislation brought few tangible benefits. The zamindari system had never allocated ownership rights to landless agricultural laborers — the status occupied by most SC households — and the provision of the 1950 Act that gave ownership rights to "occupancy tenants" excluded the majority of SC families who had no formal tenancy rights, only precarious labor relationships with landlords. Subsequent land ceiling legislation, which set maximum limits on individual landholdings and mandated the distribution of surplus land to the landless, was similarly evaded by wealthy landowners in Hardoi, and the amount of land actually redistributed to SC households was a small fraction of what the legislation envisaged (Mukherjee, 1958; Mearns, 1999; Government of Uttar Pradesh, 1975).

The result is that landlessness remains the defining characteristic of the economic condition of Scheduled Caste households in Hardoi. Data from the Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC, 2011) and the NSSO surveys consistently show that SC households in UP are overwhelmingly landless or near-landless: a large majority of SC households own less than one acre of land (primarily comprising the homestead plot and a small kitchen garden), and a substantial proportion own no agricultural land at all. In Hardoi specifically, field surveys conducted by researchers at the Giri Institute of Development Studies (Lucknow) have found that between 65 and 75 per cent of SC households in the district are functionally landless — that is, they own insufficient land to meet basic household subsistence needs from their own cultivation (Giri Institute of Development Studies, 2012; Mearns, 1999).

### 3.5.3 Agricultural Labor and Wage Relations

The landlessness of SC households in Hardoi means that the majority of SC working adults are dependent on agricultural wage labor for their primary livelihood. They work as hired laborers on the farms of large and medium farmers, who are predominantly from upper-caste (Brahmin/Thakur) or dominant-OBC (Yadav/Kurmi) communities. The terms of this labor relationship — the wage rate, the regularity of employment, the freedom to seek employment from multiple employers, and the conditions of work — are critical determinants of the economic independence and social dignity of SC households.

Agricultural wages in Hardoi have historically been suppressed by a combination of labor market conditions (the surplus of landless laborers relative to the demand for agricultural labor), social norms (the caste-based obligation of SC laborers to work for particular landlord families at below-market rates), and outright coercion. While minimum agricultural wage legislation exists in Uttar Pradesh, enforcement in rural areas is minimal, and surveys consistently find that actual wages paid to agricultural laborers in Hardoi fall below the statutory minimum in many cases (Lerche, 1999; Directorate of Economics and Statistics, UP, 2015).

The dependence of SC households on employment provided by the same upper-caste and OBC employers who constitute the dominant caste groups in the gram panchayat creates a fundamental tension for Dalit women panchayat members. When a Dalit woman representative challenges the interests of a large farmer who employs her husband or other male relatives as agricultural laborers, the economic consequences for her household can be severe. Several studies of Dalit representatives in UP PRIs have documented cases in which dominant-caste employers threatened to withdraw employment from the relatives of Dalit representatives who exercised independent political authority, effectively using economic dependence as a mechanism of political control (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Pai, 2001).

### 3.5.4 Non-Agricultural Livelihoods and Economic Diversification

Beyond agricultural labor, SC households in Hardoi are engaged in a variety of non-agricultural livelihoods, including traditional occupational activities (leatherwork, weaving, washing, sanitation), casual construction labor, petty trade, and migration for urban employment. The gradual decline of the jajmani system — the traditional arrangement by which SC service castes provided occupational services to upper-caste households in exchange for customary payments in grain — has both eroded the material security that the system provided and liberated SC households from the most oppressive forms of ritual dependence. Many younger Dalit men and women in Hardoi now seek employment in Lucknow, Kanpur, and Delhi rather than accepting the degraded terms of traditional occupational roles in their home villages (Mendelsohn & Vicziany, 1998; Pai, 2002).

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), enacted in 2005, has provided an important additional source of livelihood for SC households in Hardoi. MGNREGA guarantees a minimum of 100 days of unskilled wage employment per year to any rural household whose adult members wish to do unskilled manual work. In districts like Hardoi with high proportions of landless SC households, MGNREGA has become a significant source of income, and the management of MGNREGA funds through gram panchayats has created both opportunities and challenges for Dalit women representatives. On the one hand, the influx of MGNREGA funds has increased the financial resources nominally under panchayat control, theoretically enhancing the agency of Dalit women sarpanches. On the other hand, the large sums involved have intensified the incentives for dominant-caste members to capture panchayat leadership and divert MGNREGA wages and works to non-SC beneficiaries (Khera, 2011; Drèze & Khera, 2009).

### 3.5.5 Poverty and Economic Marginalization

The economic marginalization of SC communities in Hardoi is reflected in multiple indicators of poverty and deprivation. According to the SECC 2011, the proportion of households in the "deprived" categories was significantly higher among SC households than among other social groups. A large proportion of SC households in Hardoi were classified as living in kutcha (non-permanent, low-quality) houses with earthen walls and thatched or tin roofs; a substantial proportion had no access to piped water; and a significant proportion depended on daily casual wage labor as their primary income source (SECC, 2011; Government of Uttar Pradesh, 2006).

The Human Development Report for Uttar Pradesh (Government of UP, 2006) found that Scheduled Caste households in the state had significantly lower scores on all dimensions of the Human Development Index — including life expectancy, educational attainment, and per capita income — compared to the state average and to non-SC households. For female SC household members, these disadvantages were compounded by gender-based discrimination in access to nutrition, healthcare, and education, resulting in particularly poor outcomes on health and human capital indicators (Government of UP, 2006; Drèze & Sen, 2013).

The economic marginalization of Dalit women in Hardoi is not merely a background condition but has direct implications for their political effectiveness. Economic dependence on dominant-caste employers constrains political independence; the inability to travel (due to lack of money for transport) constrains participation in block-level meetings; low nutritional status and poor health constrain the physical capacity for political activism; and the time burdens of unpaid domestic labor and agricultural wage work constrain the time available for political activities. Understanding the economic dimension of Dalit women's lives in Hardoi is therefore essential for interpreting their political experiences.

## 3.6 EDUCATIONAL STATUS AND LITERACY PATTERNS

### 3.6.1 Overall Literacy in Hardoi

Literacy — defined in the Census of India as the ability to read and write with understanding in any language — is a fundamental indicator of human capability and a crucial determinant of the effectiveness of political participation. In the context of panchayat governance, basic literacy is essential for reading official documents, understanding government schemes, attending and contributing to gram sabha meetings, and navigating the administrative procedures of local government. The educational profile of Hardoi district, and specifically the severe literacy disadvantage of Dalit women, is therefore of direct relevance to the present study.

According to the 2011 Census, the overall literacy rate in Hardoi district was 67.5 per cent, compared to the state average of 67.7 per cent and the national average of 74.0 per cent. While this suggests that Hardoi's overall literacy performance is roughly in line with the state average, this aggregate figure conceals enormous disparities by gender, caste, and place of residence. The male literacy rate was 78.9 per cent, while the female literacy rate was only 54.7 per cent, a gap of 24.2 percentage points — reflecting the pervasive gender discrimination in access to education that characterizes rural UP (Census of India, 2011; ASER, 2018).

Rural literacy rates in Hardoi were significantly lower than the district average, with rural male literacy at approximately 76.2 per cent and rural female literacy at approximately 51.4 per cent. These rural figures are significantly lower than the national averages for rural males (77.2 per cent) and rural females (57.9 per cent), positioning Hardoi among the educationally more disadvantaged districts in the country (Census of India, 2011).

### 3.6.2 SC Literacy and the Dalit Educational Disadvantage

The literacy rates of Scheduled Caste communities in Hardoi are substantially lower than those of the general population. While district-level SC-specific literacy data from the 2011 Census are not always disaggregated in published reports, data from the UP Human Development Report and from NSSO surveys of educational attainment in UP consistently show that SC literacy rates in Hardoi and similar districts lag behind both the general population and the SC national average (Government of UP, 2006; NSSO, 2014).

For SC women specifically, the literacy disadvantage is particularly severe. In rural UP, the female literacy rate among Scheduled Castes is consistently among the lowest of any socio-demographic group, reflecting the compounded effect of caste discrimination (which restricts SC access to schools and discourages SC children's enrollment), gender discrimination (which leads families to prioritize boys' education over girls'), and poverty (which makes school attendance unaffordable when it competes with the need for children's — particularly girls' — labor in the household and in agricultural work) (Deshpande, 2011; Velaskar, 1998; ASER, 2018).

The low levels of literacy among SC women in Hardoi have direct consequences for their capacity to function as effective panchayat representatives. Several studies of Dalit women panchayat members in UP have found that illiteracy is one of the primary factors enabling the "proxy" phenomenon, in which male relatives sign official documents and attend meetings in place of the elected woman representative. When a Dalit woman sarpanch cannot read the documents placed before her for signature, she is entirely dependent on intermediaries — the panchayat secretary, her husband, or other male relatives — for the most basic administrative functions of her role, creating conditions of structural dependency that constrain her political autonomy (Pai, 2001; Mishra, 2011).

### 3.6.3 Primary and Secondary School Infrastructure

The school infrastructure in Hardoi has expanded significantly since the implementation of the Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan (SSA/Education for All) program from 2001 onwards. The district now has a relatively dense network of primary schools (Classes 1–5) in most revenue villages, with a government primary school within one kilometer of the majority of habitations. However, the quality of primary schooling in Hardoi's government schools is severely compromised by high teacher absenteeism, inadequate infrastructure, and limited pedagogical inputs, resulting in low levels of effective learning among students who do attend (ASER, 2018; Chandra, 2006).

Upper primary schools (Classes 6–8) are less densely distributed than primary schools, and the distance to the nearest upper primary school is a significant barrier to continued schooling for girls, particularly in Dalit tolas located on the periphery of villages. Secondary schools (Classes 9–10) and higher secondary schools (Classes 11–12) are concentrated in block headquarters and town centres, placing them beyond the practical reach of girls from remote Dalit households who face restrictions on mobility and cannot afford boarding accommodation in distant towns (ASER, 2018; Government of UP, 2006).

The mid-day meal scheme, implemented through primary schools in Hardoi, has been an important incentive for enrollment and retention, particularly for SC children. However, documented cases of caste discrimination in the distribution of mid-day meals — including separate seating for SC children and the refusal of upper-caste cooks to prepare food for SC students — illustrate how even well-intentioned educational interventions are subverted by the pervasive caste norms of rural UP (Human Rights Watch, 1999; Shah et al., 2006).

### 3.6.4 Higher Education and SC Access

Access to higher education (colleges and universities) is extremely limited for Scheduled Caste communities in Hardoi. While the reservation provisions in educational institutions (27 per cent for OBCs and 21 per cent for SCs in state institutions in UP) have created formal opportunities for SC students to enter higher education, the actual take-up of these opportunities by SC students from Hardoi is constrained by the inadequacy of their school-level education, the cost of higher education even with fee concessions, the geographical distance to colleges and universities (located primarily in Lucknow, Kanpur, and Allahabad), and the social norms that restrict the mobility of young women (Deshpande, 2011; Thorat & Newman, 2010).

The small but growing number of educated young Dalit women in Hardoi who have completed secondary or higher secondary education and who have entered panchayat politics represents a significant departure from the pattern of the previous generation. These educated Dalit women tend to be more effective as political representatives, more knowledgeable about their formal rights and responsibilities, and more resistant to the proxy phenomenon than their less-educated counterparts. The expansion of educational opportunities for Dalit girls, however inadequate it remains, is therefore an important structural driver of the gradual transformation in Dalit women's political participation in Hardoi (Mishra, 2011; Nambiar, 2001).

### 3.6.5 Vocational and Non-Formal Education

In addition to formal schooling, several government programs and NGOs have delivered vocational training and non-formal education to Dalit women in Hardoi. The National Rural Livelihood Mission (NRLM) and its predecessor, the Swarnajayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana (SGSY), have organized self-help groups (SHGs) for women in which basic literacy, numeracy, and livelihood skills are imparted alongside the management of microcredit. Several NGOs working in Hardoi, including organizations affiliated with Ambedkarite movements, have conducted literacy camps and awareness programs specifically targeted at Dalit women panchayat members, providing basic training in their rights, responsibilities, and administrative procedures (Narayan, 1999; Government of UP, 2006).

These non-formal educational interventions, while limited in scale, have contributed to the development of political capacity among some Dalit women representatives in Hardoi and represent an important complement to formal reservation as a mechanism of political inclusion. The experience of SHG participation in particular has been identified in several studies as a significant precursor to effective political participation among Dalit women, providing experience in collective decision-making, financial management, and public speaking that strengthens the capacity for independent political agency (Nambiar, 2001; Buch, 2000).

## 3.7 POLITICAL HISTORY AND ELECTORAL DYNAMICS OF HARDOI

### 3.7.1 The Colonial and Early Post-Independence Period

The political history of the Hardoi region stretches back to the colonial period, when the district was part of the administrative division of Lucknow in the United Provinces of Agra and Oudh. Under British rule, the dominant political actors in the region were the taluqdars — large landowners, mostly Thakur and Brahmin, who had been granted proprietary rights by the colonial government in exchange for their loyalty after the uprising of 1857. These taluqdars wielded enormous social and political influence in their localities, controlling the votes of their dependent tenants and laborers through a combination of patronage and coercion (Pandey, 1978; Mukherjee, 1958).

The nationalist movement in Hardoi was primarily a Congress affair, with the All India National Congress mobilizing both the peasant masses and sections of the urban intelligentsia against colonial rule. The contribution of Dalit communities to the independence movement in Hardoi was partly mediated through Ambedkar's organizations, which operated in the region in the 1930s and 1940s, though the Ambedkarite presence was less prominent in Hardoi than in some other parts of UP with larger Chamar populations. The post-independence constitution's reservation provisions were welcomed by Dalit communities in Hardoi as a historic step toward political inclusion, even as the social and economic structures that underpinned their subordination remained largely intact (Zelliot, 1992; Omvedt, 1994).

### 3.7.2 Congress Dominance and Dalit Politics, 1952–1989

In the first four decades of Indian democracy, the Congress party dominated electoral politics in Hardoi. The Congress's success in the district was built on a complex coalition of interests that combined the support of dominant-caste landlords (many of whom had converted from zamindars to Congress politicians through the accommodation of their interests in the land reform process), Muslim minority communities, and Dalit voters who supported Congress partly out of loyalty to Gandhi's memory and partly because the Congress controlled the state machinery that dispensed patronage and welfare resources to SC communities.

The relationship between Congress and Dalit voters in Hardoi was fundamentally patronage-based rather than empowerment-oriented. Dalit communities supported Congress in exchange for access to government ration shops, housing allotments, scholarship schemes, and other welfare benefits that the Congress government distributed through a network of local intermediaries. However, this patronage relationship was also inherently disempowering: it positioned Dalits as beneficiaries of upper-caste benevolence rather than as autonomous political agents, reinforced their economic and social dependence on dominant-caste politicians, and provided no mechanism for challenging the caste hierarchy that sustained their subordination (Pai, 2002; Hasan, 1998; Brass, 1974).

The Republican Party of India (RPI), founded by Ambedkar in 1956, had a presence in Hardoi but was unable to mount a sustained electoral challenge to Congress dominance. The RPI's organizational weaknesses, internal factionalism, and its limitation to a primarily SC social base prevented it from building the broader inter-caste coalitions needed for electoral success in constituencies where SC voters, though numerous, were not always a majority. Nevertheless, the RPI's presence in Hardoi kept the flame of Ambedkarite politics alive and provided an organizational base from which the more successful BSP mobilization of the 1990s would later build (Omvedt, 1994; Pai, 2002).

### 3.7.3 The Mandal-Kamandal Moment and the Rise of the BSP, 1989–2007

The political landscape of Hardoi was fundamentally transformed by the twin shocks of the Mandal Commission recommendations (implemented 1990) and the Ayodhya movement (Kamandal politics) in the early 1990s. The announcement by V.P. Singh's government in 1990 of the implementation of the Mandal Commission's recommendations for 27 per cent OBC reservation in central government jobs triggered both violent opposition from upper-caste students and youth and enthusiastic support from OBC and SC communities across UP, including in Hardoi. This catalytic moment accelerated the political mobilization of lower castes and provided the BSP with the ideological and organizational momentum it needed to make a decisive breakthrough (Jaffrelot, 2003; Hasan, 1998).

The BSP, founded by Kanshi Ram in 1984 and subsequently led by Mayawati, transformed Dalit politics in Hardoi by offering a fundamentally different relationship between political organization and Dalit communities. Unlike the Congress, which offered patronage without empowerment, the BSP offered political education, organizational mobilization, and the explicit goal of Dalit political rule (bahujan raj) as the path to social transformation. The BSP's message — captured in Kanshi Ram's famous slogan "Vote hamara, raj tumhara, nahi chalega, nahi chalega" (Your rule with our votes will no longer be accepted) — resonated powerfully with Dalit communities in Hardoi who had experienced decades of being taken for granted by Congress politicians (Pai, 2002; Jaffrelot, 2003).

In Hardoi specifically, the BSP's organizational expansion during the 1990s was driven by a network of dedicated cadre drawn primarily from educated Chamar youth who had benefited from SC reservation in education and government employment and had been exposed to Ambedkarite ideas through the BSP's cultural and political training programs. These cadre organized Dalit households across the district, registered them to vote, conducted voter awareness campaigns, and mobilized support for BSP candidates in assembly and local elections. The BSP's success in Hardoi — it won the Lok Sabha seat from Hardoi in 1996 and made significant gains in assembly elections — reflected this grassroots organizational effort and the deep hunger for political dignity among Dalit communities (Pai, 2002; Verma, 2005).

### 3.7.4 Mayawati's Governments and Their Impact on Hardoi

The four governments led by Mayawati as Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh (1995, 1997, 2002–03, and 2007–2012) had significant impacts on Hardoi district, both in terms of material policy outputs and in terms of the symbolic politics of Dalit dignity and assertion. Mayawati's governments implemented a range of policies particularly relevant to SC communities, including the construction of Ambedkar villages (gram where infrastructure was improved and SC household entitlements prioritized), the installation of Ambedkar statues and the renaming of roads and parks in honor of Dalit icons, the implementation of atrocity cases under the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act with greater vigor, and the appointment of Dalit officers to key administrative positions (Pai, 2002; Jaffrelot, 2003).

In Hardoi, Mayawati's governments provided a political climate in which Dalit communities felt a degree of administrative support and social recognition that was unprecedented in their post-independence experience. The ability to approach district administration offices and receive a hearing, the visible presence of Dalit officers in positions of authority, and the symbolic message of a Dalit woman as Chief Minister of UP's largest state all contributed to a transformation in the political consciousness of Dalit communities in Hardoi — and particularly of Dalit women, for whom Mayawati's political success served as the most powerful available role model (Pai, 2002; Deshpande, 2007).

However, the limits of top-down Dalit political power were also evident in Hardoi during the Mayawati era. Despite the BSP government's symbolic commitments to Dalit dignity, the structural conditions of Dalit economic dependence, low educational attainment, and social subordination in Hardoi remained largely unchanged. The capture of government development funds by dominant-caste intermediaries continued, the enforcement of Dalit land rights remained minimal, and the day-to-day practice of untouchability and caste discrimination in village public spaces continued largely unabated (Verma, 2005; Pai, 2002).

### 3.7.5 Current Electoral Landscape: SP, BJP, and the BSP's Changing Fortunes

Since the BSP's defeat in the 2012 UP state elections and the subsequent electoral dominance of first the Samajwadi Party (2012–2017) and then the BJP (2017–present), the political landscape of Hardoi has undergone significant transformation. The BSP has faced declining electoral support in Hardoi as in the rest of UP, partly due to the BJP's success in peeling away sections of non-Jatav Dalit voters through the appeal of Hindutva ideology and targeted welfarism (PM Ujjwala gas connections, PM Awas Yojana housing, Swachh Bharat toilets). The SP under Akhilesh Yadav has sought to rebuild the Yadav-Muslim coalition while also appealing to non-Yadav OBCs, but its record on Dalit issues in the 2012–2017 period was mixed (Jaffrelot, 2021; Kumar, 2017).

For Dalit women in panchayat politics in Hardoi, these shifts in the state-level political landscape have had direct consequences. The BSP's decline has reduced the political protection and administrative support that BSP-affiliated Dalit women representatives previously enjoyed. The BJP government's emphasis on competitive welfarism and Hindu unity, while materially benefiting some SC households through housing and sanitation schemes, has not challenged the social hierarchies of caste in rural Hardoi and has arguably weakened the explicit Dalit assertion politics that underpinned the BSP era. Dalit women panchayat members who were elected on BSP tickets in recent elections have had to navigate a more complex and less supportive political environment than their predecessors of the 2000s (Jaffrelot, 2021; Verma, 2005).

### 3.7.6 Assembly Constituencies and Local Electoral Data

Hardoi district contains five assembly constituencies: Hardoi, Sandila, Bilgram, Madhoganj, and Sawayajpur. Of these, the Bilgram assembly constituency is a Scheduled Caste reserved constituency, reflecting the high SC population concentration in that part of the district. The history of electoral competition in these constituencies illustrates the dynamics of caste-based politics in Hardoi: in unreserved constituencies, SC voters are a crucial swing bloc whose support has been courted by all major parties; in the reserved Bilgram constituency, SC candidates from BSP, SP, BJP, and Congress have competed for votes primarily from the SC community while also seeking support from other communities (Election Commission of India, 2017; Jaffrelot, 2003).

The Hardoi Lok Sabha constituency encompasses the entire district and has been a competitive battleground between BSP, SP, and BJP. The BSP won the Hardoi Lok Sabha seat in 1996 and 1999, reflecting the height of BSP organizational strength in the district. Since 2014, the BJP has won the Lok Sabha seat in successive elections, benefiting from the Modi wave and the fragmentation of the anti-BJP vote between BSP and SP. These state and national electoral dynamics provide the macro-political context within which panchayat elections in Hardoi take place, shaping the political affiliations, organizational resources, and social contexts of Dalit women panchayat members (Election Commission of India, 2019; Jaffrelot, 2021).

## 3.8 PANCHAYATI RAJ INSTITUTIONS IN HARDOI: STRUCTURE AND FUNCTIONING

### 3.8.1 The Three-Tier PRI Structure in Hardoi

The Panchayati Raj system in Hardoi district operates in conformity with the constitutional provisions of the 73rd Amendment and the corresponding legislation of the Uttar Pradesh Panchayati Raj Act, 1947 (as amended). The system comprises three tiers: the Gram Panchayat at the village level, the Kshetra Panchayat (Block Panchayat) at the development block level, and the Zila Panchayat at the district level. Each tier has its own elected body, with elections conducted by the State Election Commission of Uttar Pradesh at five-year intervals.

**Gram Panchayat**: The gram panchayat is the foundational unit of the Panchayati Raj system in Hardoi. Each gram panchayat covers one or more revenue villages and is governed by an elected body comprising a Gram Pradhan (village head, equivalent to sarpanch in other states) and Ward Members (panch). According to the UP Panchayati Raj Act, wards are demarcated on the basis of population, with each ward containing approximately 500 persons. As of the most recent panchayat election (2021), Hardoi district had 858 gram panchayats distributed across the 19 development blocks (District Panchayati Raj Officer, Hardoi, 2021).

The Gram Pradhan is directly elected by the entire adult electorate of the gram panchayat area in a single constituency. Ward Members are elected from individual wards. The quorum for gram panchayat meetings requires the presence of at least one-half of the total membership, including the Gram Pradhan. The gram panchayat is empowered to prepare and implement a gram panchayat development plan, maintain local infrastructure, implement welfare schemes, and levy certain taxes and fees. The Gram Sabha — comprising all adult voters in the panchayat area — is required to meet at least two times annually and must approve the gram panchayat's plans and accounts (Government of Uttar Pradesh, UP Panchayati Raj Act, 1947 as amended; Ministry of Panchayati Raj, 2014).

**Kshetra Panchayat (Block Panchayat)**: The Kshetra Panchayat is the intermediate tier of the PRI system in UP. Each of Hardoi's 19 development blocks has a Kshetra Panchayat, whose membership comprises directly elected block members (Kshetra Panchayat Sadasy) representing territorial constituencies within the block, and ex-officio members including all Gram Pradhans within the block and the MLAs and MPs whose constituencies overlap with the block. The Kshetra Panchayat is headed by a directly elected Chairman (Pramukh) and Deputy Chairman (Sahayak Pramukh). The Kshetra Panchayat is responsible for planning and implementing development activities at the block level, coordinating the activities of gram panchayats, and managing certain government programs devolved to the block level (Government of Uttar Pradesh, UP Panchayati Raj Act; District Panchayati Raj Officer, Hardoi, 2021).

**Zila Panchayat**: The Zila Panchayat is the apex body of the Panchayati Raj system in Hardoi district. It is governed by a directly elected board of Zila Panchayat members (one from each territorial constituency in the district) and headed by a directly elected Adhyaksha (President). The Zila Panchayat is responsible for district-level planning and coordination, the maintenance of district roads and other infrastructure, and the implementation of certain centrally sponsored and state government schemes. Its budget is funded through a combination of state government grants, own tax revenues, and transfers from the Finance Commission (Government of Uttar Pradesh, UP Panchayati Raj Act; Ministry of Panchayati Raj, 2014).

### 3.8.2 Reservation Provisions in Hardoi's PRIs

The reservation provisions mandated by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment and the UP Panchayati Raj Act have significantly shaped the composition of elected bodies at all three tiers in Hardoi. The principal provisions are:

- **Women's Reservation**: Not less than one-third of all seats (including the positions of Gram Pradhan and Kshetra Panchayat Chairman) are reserved for women. In UP, the state government has enhanced this to 50 per cent in 2020 onwards, meaning that half of all gram panchayat seats and half of all Gram Pradhan positions are now reserved for women across the state (Government of Uttar Pradesh, 2020).

- **SC Reservation**: Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes in proportion to their population in the panchayat area. In Hardoi district, where the SC population constitutes approximately 29.7 per cent of the total, approximately 30 per cent of all gram panchayat seats are reserved for SC candidates. Among the SC-reserved positions, at least one-third (now one-half under the 2020 enhancement) must be reserved for SC women.

- **OBC Reservation**: Seats are reserved for Other Backward Classes, and among OBC-reserved seats, one-third (now one-half) must be reserved for OBC women.

- **Rotation of Reserved Constituencies**: Reserved constituencies are rotated at each election to different wards and gram panchayats, so that no single village or ward is permanently reserved or permanently unreserved. This rotation system is intended to prevent the entrenchment of reserved representatives in particular positions and to ensure broader distribution of representation across the district (UP Panchayati Raj Act, as amended; District Panchayati Raj Officer, Hardoi, 2021).

The interaction of women's reservation and SC reservation creates the specific category of "SC women's reservation" that is central to the present study. In Hardoi's gram panchayats, those positions that fall in the intersection of the women's and SC reservations are designated for SC women candidates only. These positions — and the women elected to them — are the primary focus of the present research.

### 3.8.3 The Panchayat Election Process in Hardoi

Panchayat elections in Hardoi are conducted by the State Election Commission of UP, which determines the schedule, the reservation roster for each election, and the rules for candidate eligibility. Elections are held on a fixed five-year cycle, with voting conducted by electronic voting machine (EVM) in a single day per block (different blocks vote on different days). Candidate nomination, campaigning, and polling are all conducted simultaneously across the state, making the logistical demands on election administration enormous.

The political parties that contest panchayat elections in Hardoi do so on party symbols in the case of the Gram Pradhan position (which is a single-member constituency), while Ward Member elections are formally non-partisan (candidates do not use party symbols) but are in practice organized along party lines, with BSP, SP, BJP, and Congress all fielding informal slates of ward member candidates alongside their Gram Pradhan nominees. The degree to which Dalit women candidates are selected for reserved Gram Pradhan and Ward Member positions by political parties, and the degree of support they receive from their parties once elected, varies considerably across parties and across the political cycle (Pai, 2002; Verma, 2005; District Panchayati Raj Officer, Hardoi, 2021).

### 3.8.4 Financial Resources of Gram Panchayats in Hardoi

The financial resources available to gram panchayats in Hardoi come from multiple sources, each with its own conditions and administrative requirements. The principal sources are:

**Finance Commission Grants**: The 14th and 15th Finance Commissions have devolved substantial funds directly to gram panchayats, to be used for specified purposes (primarily the provision of basic services such as drinking water, sanitation, roads, and street lighting). In Hardoi, Finance Commission grants constitute the largest single source of untied funds for most gram panchayats, providing resources that are nominally under the control of the elected Gram Pradhan (Ministry of Finance, Government of India, 2021; Ministry of Panchayati Raj, 2019).

**MGNREGA**: As noted above, MGNREGA funds flowing through gram panchayats for the implementation of public works are a significant financial resource. In Hardoi's gram panchayats, annual MGNREGA expenditures can range from a few lakhs to several crore rupees depending on the number of workers registered and the labor demands in a given year (Ministry of Rural Development, 2021; Khera, 2011).

**Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana – Gramin (PMAY-G)**: This housing scheme, which provides financial assistance to eligible rural households for the construction of permanent houses, channels significant funds through gram panchayats in Hardoi, given the high proportion of SC households in kutcha housing. The identification of beneficiaries and the certification of house completion are functions of the gram panchayat, giving the Gram Pradhan significant discretionary power over who receives housing assistance (Ministry of Rural Development, 2021).

**Swachh Bharat Mission – Gramin (SBM-G)**: The individual household latrine construction program has been a major source of panchayat activity in Hardoi since 2014, with significant funds flowing through panchayats for toilet construction and open-defecation-free (ODF) certification (Ministry of Jal Shakti, 2021).

**State Government Grants**: The state government provides various grants to gram panchayats for infrastructure maintenance, officer salaries, and special development projects. The allocation of these grants is often influenced by the political affiliations of Gram Pradhans, with panchayats led by MLAs' preferred candidates tending to receive better allocations (Saxena, 1994; Lieten & Srivastava, 1999).

Despite this multiplicity of funding sources, the actual financial autonomy of gram panchayats in Hardoi is severely constrained. Most funds come with conditions attached (tied grants), must be spent on specific items, and are released in tranches contingent on utilization reports. The administrative capacity of gram panchayats to manage these financial flows — prepare utilization reports, maintain accounts, meet audit requirements — is extremely limited, particularly in panchayats led by Dalit women representatives with low educational attainment and no prior administrative experience (Mathew & Nayak, 1996; Singh & Sharma, 2007).

### 3.8.5 The Gram Panchayat Secretary: A Critical Administrative Interface

The Gram Panchayat Secretary (Sachiv in Hindi) is a government-employed functionary who serves as the administrative backbone of the gram panchayat. The Sachiv is responsible for maintaining the gram panchayat's accounts and records, drafting official documents and correspondence, implementing government directives, and interfacing with block and district level offices on behalf of the panchayat. The Sachiv is typically a literate or graduate-level government employee who may have significant administrative experience and knowledge of government procedures.

In the context of Dalit women's representation in Hardoi's gram panchayats, the Sachiv occupies a pivotal and often problematic role. When the elected Gram Pradhan is a Dalit woman with limited education and administrative knowledge, she is highly dependent on the Sachiv for even the most basic administrative functions of her role. The Sachiv, who is often from a dominant caste, may exploit this dependency to exercise de facto control over panchayat decisions, diverting funds, manipulating accounts, and bypassing the Pradhan's formal authority. Alternatively, the Sachiv may simply neglect his duties or be rarely present at the panchayat bhavan — a common complaint of gram panchayat members in UP — effectively rendering the panchayat administratively dysfunctional (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Pai, 2001; Mishra, 2011).

Studies of Dalit women panchayat members in UP have consistently identified the Sachiv as a key figure in the political environment that shapes the effectiveness of Dalit women's representation. Those Dalit women representatives who have managed to establish a working relationship with a cooperative Sachiv — or who have been able to employ their own trusted associate as Sachiv — have generally been more effective in their formal roles than those who have faced an uncooperative or corrupt Sachiv (Lieten & Srivastava, 1999; Buch, 2000).

### 3.8.6 The Gram Sabha in Hardoi: Theory and Practice

The Gram Sabha — the assembly of all adult voters in the gram panchayat area — is constitutionally the foundational democratic institution of the Panchayati Raj system and the primary accountability mechanism for the gram panchayat. Under the UP Panchayati Raj Act, Gram Sabhas are required to be held at least twice annually (typically on Independence Day and Republic Day), and must approve the gram panchayat's annual development plan, the list of beneficiaries for government welfare schemes, and the completion reports for public works.

In practice, however, Gram Sabha meetings in Hardoi rarely fulfill their constitutional and legal potential. Empirical studies of Gram Sabhas in UP consistently find that attendance at these meetings is low, dominated by male upper-caste members, and that agenda-setting and decision-making are controlled by the Gram Pradhan and dominant-caste influencers rather than by the mass of the electorate. Dalit households — and Dalit women in particular — are typically marginalized within Gram Sabha proceedings through a combination of physical exclusion (the practice of holding meetings in upper-caste-controlled spaces), social intimidation, and the deliberate timing of meetings during periods of peak agricultural labor when Dalit women are occupied with wage work (Jha, Rao, & Woolcock, 2007; Aiyar, Mehta, & Samji, 2007).

When the Gram Pradhan herself is a Dalit woman, the dynamics of Gram Sabha participation are modified in complex ways. On the one hand, a Dalit woman Pradhan may make deliberate efforts to hold meetings in more accessible locations, to actively invite Dalit community members to attend, and to create a more inclusive deliberative environment. On the other hand, she may face resistance and obstruction from dominant-caste members who refuse to accept her authority to preside over the meeting. The evidence from field studies suggests that the quality of Gram Sabha functioning varies enormously depending on the individual characteristics of the Pradhan, the political context of the village, and the degree of collective organization of the Dalit community (Rao, 2008; Buch, 2005).

## 3.9 STATUS OF SCHEDULED CASTE COMMUNITIES IN HARDOI

### 3.9.1 Socioeconomic Conditions of SC Households

The socioeconomic conditions of Scheduled Caste communities in Hardoi are among the most disadvantaged in the state. Drawing on data from the Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC, 2011), the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), and field surveys conducted by the Giri Institute of Development Studies and other research organizations, a composite picture of SC deprivation in Hardoi can be constructed across multiple dimensions.

**Housing**: The vast majority of SC households in Hardoi continue to live in kutcha or semi-pucca housing, with earthen walls and thatched or corrugated tin roofs. While the PMAY-G housing scheme has provided permanent houses to some SC beneficiaries in recent years, field surveys suggest that the targeting of the scheme has been uneven and that many of the most deprived SC households — particularly those in remote Dalit tolas — have not benefited due to lack of awareness, inability to navigate the application process, or active exclusion by dominant-caste dominated panchayats (SECC, 2011; Ministry of Rural Development, 2021).

**Drinking Water and Sanitation**: Access to safe drinking water remains inadequate for many SC households in Hardoi. While government handpumps have been installed in most Dalit tolas, a significant proportion of these handpumps are non-functional at any given time due to poor maintenance. More significantly, several documented cases in Hardoi attest to the practice of restricting Dalit access to village wells, ponds, and common water sources — a direct manifestation of untouchability that continues despite constitutional prohibition (Shah et al., 2006; Human Rights Watch, 1999). The Swachh Bharat Mission has improved toilet access in SC households, but open defecation remains prevalent in some areas due to incomplete coverage and persistent behavioral habits.

**Income and Employment**: SC households in Hardoi are overwhelmingly concentrated in the lowest income quintiles. A large proportion depend on agricultural wage labor, MGNREGA public works, and traditional occupational activities (including leatherwork, sanitation labor, and weaving) as their primary income sources. The National Sample Survey's employment data consistently shows that SC workers in UP face lower wages, fewer working days, and higher rates of unemployment than non-SC workers, reflecting the compounded effects of discrimination, lower skill levels (due to educational exclusion), and limited access to credit and productive assets (NSSO, 2014; Thorat & Newman, 2010).

### 3.9.2 Untouchability Practices in Hardoi Villages

Despite more than seven decades of constitutional prohibition and the legal protections of the Protection of Civil Rights Act (1955) and the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (1989, amended 2015), untouchability practices continue to be observed in many villages of Hardoi district. Shah et al.'s (2006) survey *Untouchability in Rural India* documented the persistence of untouchability practices across rural UP, including Hardoi, and more recent field studies confirm that these practices, while gradually diminishing in urban and peri-urban areas, remain prevalent in many rural villages.

Common untouchability practices documented in Hardoi villages include:
- The maintenance of separate seating arrangements at public functions, with Dalit community members seated separately from upper-caste and OBC members;
- The denial of equal access to village wells, ponds, and water sources to Dalit households;
- The requirement that Dalit workers receive tea and food in separate vessels (or to bring their own vessels) when hired for agricultural or domestic work in upper-caste households;
- The practice of "two-finger" (do-ungali) customs in tea shops, where Dalit customers receive tea without the cup touching the server's hands;
- The requirement that Dalit community members remove footwear when passing through the upper-caste residential area of the village;
- The prohibition on Dalits entering temples and participating equally in village religious ceremonies;
- The practice of "barefoot justice" (Dalit complainants being required to stand before upper-caste panchayat members without footwear);
- The social boycott (samajik bahishkar) of Dalit households or individuals who challenge caste norms or assert their rights (Shah et al., 2006; Human Rights Watch, 1999; Singh, 2003).

These everyday manifestations of untouchability are directly relevant to the political experiences of Dalit women panchayat members in Hardoi. When a Dalit woman Gram Pradhan is required to preside over a gram sabha or gram panchayat meeting, the social norms of untouchability that govern interaction in village public spaces create immediate practical challenges: upper-caste members may refuse to sit in proximity to her, may refuse to accept documents she has handled, or may address her in degrading terms that undermine her formal authority. The persistence of untouchability thus constitutes a form of structural resistance to Dalit women's political empowerment that operates beneath and alongside the formal institutional structures of the panchayat system.

### 3.9.3 Land Rights and Dalit Housing Settlements

A specific dimension of the SC condition in Hardoi that is particularly relevant to panchayat politics is the question of Dalit housing land (abadi land). In many villages of Hardoi, Dalit households' claims to the residential land on which their houses are built are legally insecure — many families have lived on a particular plot for generations but lack formal land titles, making them vulnerable to eviction by dominant-caste landowners or to encroachment on their plots by more powerful neighbors. The securing of formal land titles (pattas) for Dalit housing plots is a key issue in gram panchayat politics and one in which Dalit women Gram Pradhans can, if they exercise independent authority, make a significant and direct difference to the lives of their constituents (Buch, 2005; Deshpande, 2007).

The government of Uttar Pradesh has, at various points, issued directives to district administrations and gram panchayats to conduct special surveys of Dalit housing land and to issue formal pattas to those households with insecure tenure. In Hardoi, the implementation of these directives has been uneven: in villages where the Gram Pradhan is from a dominant caste, Dalit housing land surveys are typically either not conducted or conducted in a manner that favors the interests of dominant-caste landowners. In some villages where Dalit women Gram Pradhans have exercised genuine authority, formal land titles have been issued to Dalit households — an achievement that some Dalit women representatives in UP cite as among their most significant accomplishments (Buch, 2005; Singh, 2003).

### 3.9.4 Access to Government Welfare Schemes

The delivery of government welfare schemes to SC beneficiaries through gram panchayats is a critical dimension of the political economy of panchayat governance in Hardoi. The principal schemes relevant to SC households include:

- **PM Awas Yojana – Gramin**: Housing assistance for households in permanent housing deprivation;
- **MGNREGA**: Employment guarantee;
- **Scholarship schemes**: Pre-matric and post-matric scholarships for SC students;
- **Pension schemes**: Old-age pension (NSAP), widow pension, and disability pension for eligible SC beneficiaries;
- **Ration card and food security**: Below-Poverty-Line (BPL) ration cards under the National Food Security Act;
- **Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana**: Free LPG connections for SC/BPL women;
- **Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana**: Financial inclusion through bank accounts;
- **Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN)**: Direct income support for small and marginal farmers (Government of India, various years; District Administration Hardoi, 2020).

The targeting and delivery of these schemes at the village level is significantly influenced by the political dynamics of the gram panchayat. Gram Pradhans have formal or informal roles in identifying beneficiaries, certifying eligibility, and facilitating the delivery of scheme benefits. When the Gram Pradhan is from a dominant caste, the targeting of these schemes is systematically biased toward non-SC beneficiaries or toward favored SC households that are politically aligned with the dominant-caste network. When the Gram Pradhan is a Dalit woman who exercises genuine authority, the targeting can be significantly more equitable, with SC households receiving a fairer share of scheme benefits (Dunning & Nilekani, 2013; Buch, 2005).

### 3.9.5 The Dalit Movement in Hardoi: Ambedkarite Organizations and BSP Networks

The Dalit community in Hardoi has a history of political and social mobilization that predates the BSP and draws on the broader tradition of Ambedkarite social reform. Various Ambedkarite organizations — including the Buddhist Society of India, the Republican Party of India (in its various factions), the Dalit Shoshit Samaj Sangharsh Samiti (DS4, founded by Kanshi Ram as a precursor to the BSP), and the BSP itself — have been active in Hardoi since at least the 1970s (Omvedt, 1994; Pai, 2002).

The BSP's organizational presence in Hardoi remains significant despite its electoral decline since 2012. The party maintains a network of sector incharges (booth-level organizers), zone incharges, and district-level office-bearers who continue to work with Dalit communities on political education, voter registration, and support for BSP-affiliated candidates in panchayat elections. This organizational network provides an important source of political support and information for Dalit women panchayat members who are affiliated with the BSP, and distinguishes the political situation of BSP-aligned Dalit women from that of unaffiliated or Congress/SP-affiliated Dalit women who lack access to a comparable organizational resource (Pai, 2002; Verma, 2005).

## 3.10 DALIT WOMEN IN HARDOI: A PROFILE OF DISADVANTAGE AND AGENCY

### 3.10.1 The Intersectional Position of Dalit Women in Hardoi

Dalit women in Hardoi occupy a position of compounded disadvantage that is defined by the intersection of caste subordination and gender oppression. As argued in Chapter 2, the concept of intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989; Collins, 1990) provides the analytical framework for understanding this position: the disadvantages experienced by Dalit women are not merely the sum of the disadvantages of being Dalit and the disadvantages of being a woman, but involve specific forms of exclusion, exploitation, and violence that are characteristic of the intersection of these two axes of social hierarchy (Rege, 2006; Guru, 1995).

In Hardoi, this intersectional position is manifest across all dimensions of social life. Dalit women face caste-based discrimination in the labor market (receiving lower wages than both non-Dalit women and Dalit men for the same agricultural work), in access to government services (being systematically excluded from information about and access to welfare schemes), in physical security (facing the specific threat of sexual violence as a mechanism of caste control), and in the most intimate dimensions of domestic life (being subject to patriarchal authority within their households while simultaneously facing the authority of caste hierarchies in the village public sphere). At the same time, Dalit women are not merely passive victims of these intersecting oppressions but are active agents who have historically organized, resisted, and sought to transform the conditions of their lives (Omvedt, 1993; Datar, 1993; Guru, 1995).

### 3.10.2 Health and Nutrition Status of Dalit Women in Hardoi

The health and nutrition status of Dalit women in Hardoi reflects the severe material deprivation of SC communities and the additional burden of gender discrimination in access to household resources. Data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21) for Uttar Pradesh show that rural SC women have significantly worse health outcomes than the state average and than non-SC women on virtually every measured indicator.

Maternal mortality rates among SC women in UP are substantially higher than the state average, reflecting the limited access of Dalit women to antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and emergency obstetric care. The proportion of SC women who deliver in institutions (hospitals and health centres) remains lower than the state average in Hardoi, as SC women in remote Dalit tolas face barriers of distance, cost, and social discrimination in public healthcare facilities. Studies have documented cases in which Dalit women have been refused treatment or treated with contempt in government hospitals in UP, reinforcing the tendency of SC households to rely on traditional birth attendants and informal practitioners (NFHS-5, 2019–21; Drèze & Sen, 2013).

Nutritional status is severely compromised among Dalit women in Hardoi. The NFHS-5 found that approximately 50–55 per cent of rural SC women in UP were anaemic, compared to the national average of 57 per cent for all women. While anaemia rates are high across all social groups in rural UP, SC women face the double disadvantage of household poverty (limiting dietary diversity) and intra-household gender discrimination in food allocation (the common practice of women eating after male household members, and eating smaller portions). Chronic anaemia has serious consequences for women's physical capacity, cognitive function, and resilience — all of which are relevant to their ability to perform the demanding physical and cognitive tasks associated with political representation (NFHS-5, 2019–21; Drèze & Khera, 2000).

### 3.10.3 Marriage, Household Relations, and Patriarchal Norms

Marriage patterns and household relations among Dalit communities in Hardoi reflect both the universal patriarchal norms of rural UP and the specific cultural traditions of different Dalit caste groups. Most Dalit women in Hardoi are married in early or mid-adolescence — child marriage, while legally prohibited, remains prevalent in rural areas of the district — and marriage typically involves the patrilocal transfer of women from their natal village to their husband's village. This means that women who take up panchayat positions in their husband's village may lack the social networks, local knowledge, and family support that are essential for effective political functioning (NFHS-5, 2019–21; Kishor, 1993).

Within the household, Dalit women in Hardoi typically occupy a subordinate position, with decision-making authority concentrated in the hands of the male household head and his senior male relatives. Women's autonomy in major decisions — including decisions about their own health, mobility, and political participation — is severely constrained by patriarchal norms that are reinforced by the economic dependence of the household on male labor income. The "sarpanch pati" phenomenon documented in the broader literature (Pai, 2001; Buch, 2000) has its specific expression in Hardoi, where many Gram Pradhans (both SC and non-SC women) are nominally represented by their husbands in panchayat affairs (Pai, 2001; Mishra, 2011).

The norm of purdah — while associated primarily with upper-caste Hindu and Muslim women — also affects Dalit women in Hardoi in modified forms. While Dalit women do not typically observe strict physical seclusion, they are subject to social norms that constrain their mobility outside the household, their interaction with unrelated men, and their presence in village public spaces. When a Dalit woman is elected to a panchayat position that requires her to regularly attend meetings, interact with male officials, and exercise authority in public, these norms create significant social costs and family pressures (Kaushik, 1993; Pai, 2001).

### 3.10.4 Sexual Violence as a Mechanism of Caste Control

One of the most disturbing dimensions of the position of Dalit women in Hardoi is the specific vulnerability to sexual violence that arises from their intersectional position at the bottom of both the caste and gender hierarchies. Sexual violence against Dalit women in rural UP — including rape, sexual assault, molestation, and forced prostitution — is not merely random violence but functions as a specific instrument of caste control, deployed to punish Dalit communities that challenge dominant-caste authority, to deter Dalit political assertion, and to maintain the social subordination of Dalit women (Datar, 1993; Human Rights Watch, 1999).

Data from the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) consistently show that UP reports among the highest numbers of atrocity cases against Dalit women in the country, and that these cases are concentrated in districts with high SC populations and active Dalit political assertion — including Hardoi (NCRB, 2019). Field studies in Hardoi have documented cases of sexual violence against Dalit women that were explicitly motivated by the victims' or their families' political activities, including support for BSP candidates, assertion of MGNREGA wage rights, or challenge to dominant-caste control of gram panchayat resources (Singh, 2003; Human Rights Watch, 1999).

For Dalit women panchayat members in Hardoi, this context of sexual violence creates a specific and pervasive threat that shapes their political behavior. The awareness that asserting independent political authority may expose them or their female relatives to sexual violence is a powerful deterrent that constrains the exercise of genuine political agency. Several Dalit women panchayat members interviewed in UP field studies have reported receiving explicit sexual threats from dominant-caste men when they attempted to exercise their formal authority, and some have capitulated to dominant-caste demands precisely to avoid these risks (Pai, 2001; Deshpande, 2007).

### 3.10.5 Dalit Women's Organizations and Social Movements in Hardoi

Despite the formidable constraints they face, Dalit women in Hardoi have a tradition of collective resistance and organization that has been growing in strength since the 1990s. Several organizational forms have been important:

**BSP's Mahila Wing**: The BSP has organized Dalit women through its Mahila Wing, which conducts meetings, awareness programs, and voter mobilization campaigns targeting Dalit women. In the period of BSP's greatest strength in Hardoi (roughly 1995–2012), the Mahila Wing provided a platform through which Dalit women could develop political consciousness, articulate their specific concerns, and build networks of solidarity with other Dalit women representatives (Pai, 2002; Verma, 2005).

**Self-Help Groups (SHGs)**: The proliferation of women's SHGs in Hardoi under various government programs (SGSY, NRLM) has created informal spaces in which Dalit women — and other marginalized women — have collectively managed microfinance, addressed household economic problems, and gradually developed the confidence and organizational capacity that can translate into political effectiveness. Studies of SHG participants in UP have found that women with SHG experience are more confident in public settings, more knowledgeable about government schemes, and more likely to assert their rights than women without such experience (Narayan, 1999; Buch, 2000).

**Ambedkarite Cultural Organizations**: In Hardoi, as in other parts of Awadh, Ambedkarite cultural organizations — including clubs, libraries, and cultural societies that celebrate Dalit history and promote Ambedkar's ideas — have provided spaces where Dalit identity is affirmed and political consciousness cultivated. These organizations typically hold events on significant dates in the Ambedkarite calendar (Dr. Ambedkar Jayanti on April 14, Mahaparinirvana Diwas on December 6, the anniversary of the Poona Pact) that serve as occasions for community solidarity and political education (Omvedt, 1994; Pai, 2002).

**NGO Support**: Several non-governmental organizations working in Hardoi — including organizations supported by national networks working on Dalit rights and women's empowerment — have conducted training programs, legal aid camps, and capacity-building workshops specifically targeted at Dalit women panchayat members. These organizations have provided crucial support to Dalit women representatives who face dominant-caste resistance, helping them understand their legal rights, file complaints under the Atrocities Act, and access administrative remedies for violations of their authority as elected representatives (Narayan, 1999; Ghosh, 2006).

### 3.10.6 Dalit Women's Entry into Panchayat Politics: Scale and Pattern

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment and the Uttar Pradesh Panchayati Raj Act created the formal conditions for Dalit women's entry into gram panchayat politics in Hardoi. In the panchayat elections held since the amendment came into force (1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2021), tens of thousands of Dalit women have been elected as Gram Pradhans and Ward Members in Hardoi's 858 gram panchayats.

In the most recent panchayat elections (2021), the enhancement of women's reservation to 50 per cent in UP meant that approximately 429 of the 858 Gram Pradhan positions in Hardoi were reserved for women, and of these, approximately 127–130 were reserved specifically for SC women (reflecting the SC population proportion of approximately 30 per cent of total reserved positions). In addition to these Gram Pradhan positions, a large number of Ward Member positions were reserved for SC women, resulting in an overall figure of several thousand Dalit women holding some form of elected panchayat office in Hardoi district (District Panchayati Raj Officer, Hardoi, 2021; Government of Uttar Pradesh, 2020).

This scale of formal political inclusion — thousands of Dalit women holding elected positions — represents a historic departure from the pre-1993 situation of near-total exclusion of Dalit women from formal governance. Even if many of these representatives function under severe constraints and exercise limited independent authority, their formal presence in the gram panchayat constitutes a symbolically significant assertion of Dalit women's political citizenship. The present study seeks to understand, through in-depth qualitative research, the range of experiences that Dalit women in Hardoi's gram panchayats have had — from those who have effectively functioned as proxies to those who have exercised genuine independent authority and contributed to tangible changes in the governance and social conditions of their communities.

### 3.10.7 Emerging Voices: The New Generation of Dalit Women Political Actors

A particularly significant development in Hardoi's political landscape in recent years has been the emergence of a new generation of younger, more educated Dalit women who are entering panchayat politics with greater political awareness, organizational backing, and assertiveness than their predecessors. This new generation — many of whom have completed secondary or higher secondary education, participated in BSP or other political organizations, and grown up with exposure to Ambedkarite ideology through family networks and community organizations — represents a qualitatively different political actor from the first-generation Dalit women panchayat members of the late 1990s and early 2000s (Pai, 2002; Mishra, 2011; Verma, 2005).

These emerging Dalit women political actors in Hardoi are characterized by:
- Higher educational attainment, enabling them to read documents and navigate administrative procedures independently;
- Prior organizational experience through BSP, SHGs, or NGO-supported networks;
- A stronger sense of political identity rooted in Ambedkarite ideology and the visible role model of Mayawati's political leadership;
- Greater awareness of their formal rights and the legal protections available to them under the Atrocities Act and other legislation;
- Social networks that connect them to other Dalit women representatives, to BSP or other party structures, and to NGOs and civil society organizations that can provide support;
- A more confrontational stance toward dominant-caste resistance to their authority.

The present study documents both the structural constraints and the political capacities of Dalit women representatives in Hardoi, attending to the diversity of experiences within this population and the factors that differentiate those who have been more effective in their roles from those who have been more constrained.

## 3.11 SUMMARY AND SIGNIFICANCE FOR THE STUDY

### 3.11.1 Synthesis of the Socio-Political Landscape

The foregoing analysis has assembled a comprehensive picture of the socio-political landscape of Hardoi district, attending to its geographical setting, demographic composition, caste structure, economic organization, educational profile, political history, institutional framework, and the specific conditions of Dalit women within this landscape. Several key themes emerge from this analysis that are central to the empirical inquiry that follows.

**First, structural inequality is deeply embedded and multi-dimensional.** The disadvantages experienced by Dalit women in Hardoi are not the product of a single cause but reflect the convergence of multiple structural conditions — landlessness, low educational attainment, patriarchal household relations, caste-based discrimination in public life, political marginalization, and the threat of violence — that reinforce and amplify each other. No single policy intervention — including reservation in gram panchayats — can address all dimensions of this structural inequality simultaneously. The present study proceeds from the premise that the impact of reservation on Dalit women's lives and on broader social change must be understood against the backdrop of this multi-dimensional structural inequality, rather than in isolation from it.

**Second, the legacy of caste hierarchy is reproduced through everyday practices, not merely formal structures.** The analysis of caste relations in Hardoi reveals that the hierarchies of the pre-independence period have been adapted rather than eliminated in the democratic era. Dominant castes have deployed formal democratic institutions — including gram panchayats — as arenas for the reproduction of their social and economic dominance, using the mechanisms of economic dependence, social intimidation, administrative manipulation, and violence to maintain their authority even in the face of formal institutional reform. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for interpreting the political experiences of Dalit women representatives in Hardoi.

**Third, the political landscape is dynamic and contested.** The rise of the BSP and the broader transformation of Dalit politics in Hardoi since the 1990s demonstrate that the social hierarchies of the caste order are not fixed and immutable but are subject to challenge, negotiation, and partial transformation through organized political action. The Mayawati era created real changes in the political consciousness and practical circumstances of Dalit communities in Hardoi, even as the deeper structural conditions remained largely intact. The current period of BSP decline and BJP dominance presents new challenges for Dalit political assertion, requiring Dalit women representatives to navigate a more complex and less supportive political environment than that of the BSP's heyday.

**Fourth, Dalit women occupy a uniquely disadvantaged — and uniquely significant — position.** The specific intersectional position of Dalit women in Hardoi — at the bottom of both the caste and gender hierarchies — means that their entry into formal political life represents a particularly profound disruption of the established social order. When a Dalit woman occupies the position of Gram Pradhan — presiding over meetings, signing official documents, directing the allocation of public resources — she challenges simultaneously the caste hierarchy that denies Dalits equal civic standing and the gender hierarchy that denies women authority in the public sphere. It is precisely because this challenge is so fundamental that the resistance to Dalit women's political authority in Hardoi is so intense.

**Fifth, agency exists alongside constraint.** The documentation of structural inequality and dominant-caste resistance throughout this chapter should not obscure the evidence of Dalit women's political agency and the real, if partial, transformations that their political participation has produced. The emergence of a new generation of educated, organized, and politically conscious Dalit women in Hardoi — building on the organizational foundations laid by the BSP, the self-help group movement, and Ambedkarite cultural organizations — represents a genuine and growing political force. The present study seeks to document and analyze both the constraints and the agency of these women, resisting the tendency toward either an overly optimistic narrative of linear empowerment or an overly pessimistic narrative of structural determinism.

### 3.11.2 The Significance of Hardoi as a Research Site

The selection of Hardoi district as the site for the present research was motivated by several considerations that the foregoing analysis has substantiated:

**High SC concentration**: With approximately 30 per cent of its population belonging to Scheduled Castes — significantly above both the state and national averages — Hardoi provides a research site where the politics of SC reservation in PRIs are particularly salient and where the numerical strength of SC communities creates both the conditions for political assertion and the incentives for dominant-caste resistance.

**Awadh regional specificity**: Hardoi's location in the Awadh region gives the research a specific regional character — the legacies of taluqdari landlordism, the culture of Awadhi social relations, and the particular history of Ambedkarite and BSP mobilization in this region — that distinguishes the findings from those of studies conducted in other regions of UP or in other states.

**The BSP factor**: The strong BSP organizational presence in Hardoi, combined with the district's significant history of Dalit political mobilization, provides a context in which Dalit women representatives include both those who have benefited from BSP organizational support and those who have operated without such support, enabling the research to examine the mediating role of party affiliation in Dalit women's political effectiveness.

**Accessibility and prior research**: Several prior studies of caste relations, agrarian structure, and panchayat functioning in Hardoi and the neighboring Awadh districts provide a foundational knowledge base that situates the present research within an established body of scholarly inquiry. At the same time, the specific focus on Dalit women as the primary unit of analysis — an approach not taken in previous studies of Hardoi — ensures that the present research makes a distinctive scholarly contribution.

**Representativeness of the Hindi belt challenge**: Hardoi district, as argued throughout this chapter, exemplifies the challenges faced by Dalit women in gram panchayats across the Hindi belt of northern India — the combination of deeply patriarchal social norms, intense caste-based hierarchies, weak PRIs, and limited civil society support. The findings from Hardoi are thus expected to have significance beyond the specific district, illuminating the conditions and possibilities of reservation-based political inclusion in some of the most challenging social environments in India.

### 3.11.3 Linking the Contextual Analysis to the Research Questions

The socio-political landscape documented in this chapter provides the specific contextual backdrop against which the research questions of the present study are addressed. The central research question — how does reservation in rural local self-government shape the lived experiences, political agency, and potential for social change among Dalit women representatives in Hardoi district? — can now be understood as a question about the interaction between the formal institutional provisions of reservation and the specific socio-political conditions described in this chapter.

The subsequent empirical chapters will draw on this contextual foundation to interpret the narratives and experiences of Dalit women panchayat members in Hardoi. The documented patterns of caste hierarchy, patriarchal authority, economic dependence, educational disadvantage, political mobilization, and institutional functioning identified in this chapter provide the interpretive framework within which the individual experiences of Dalit women representatives can be situated and understood. The present chapter thus serves both as a scholarly account of Hardoi's socio-political conditions and as an essential analytical resource for the empirical inquiry that follows.
